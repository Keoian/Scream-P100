THE LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER'S LAST LOG

The tide table said low water at 4:12, but the sea in Penhallow Sound had never read the tables, and so Ysolde Marrick stood on the gallery rail at half past three with a mug of tea going cold between her mittens, watching the water draw itself back from the rocks like a held breath.

She had kept the light at Carrow Skerry for thirty-one years. For the last nineteen of them the light had been automated, which meant that a small gray box in the lamp room decided when the beam turned and a cheerful man from the Northern Lights Board came out twice a year in a boat that smelled of diesel to check that the box still agreed with itself. Ysolde stayed anyway. Nobody had formally asked her to, and nobody had formally asked her to leave. The Board's paperwork described her as "resident caretaker, non-operational," a phrase she had once read aloud to the gulls and then never repeated.

This morning the paperwork was going to catch up with her. The cheerful man, whose name was Declan Oyelaran and who had a wife and a mortgage and a habit of whistling hymns when he was nervous, had telephoned the night before. The Board was selling the station. A hotel company wanted it. There would be, he said, a generous settlement, and he whistled a verse of "Eternal Father, Strong to Save" before he hung up, which told her more than the words had.

She finished the tea anyway, because cold tea was still tea, and went in to do what she had done every morning since she was twenty-six: she opened the log.

The log was not required. The gray box kept its own record in some chip she had never seen. But Ysolde's predecessor, a taciturn Orcadian named Halvard Tegg, had left her a fat green ledger with a cracked spine and an instruction written on the flyleaf in pencil: "Write what the sea did, not what you felt about it. Feelings are for letters." She had followed the first half of that advice for three decades and ignored the second only when she was alone, which was always.

She turned to the day's page. Under the date she wrote: Wind SW force 4, backing. Visibility good, six miles. Swell two feet from the west with a longer one underneath it from somewhere farther off. A trawler, the Mary Concannon, passing north at 0640, no signals. Then she sat with the pen above the paper for some time, because she had never in thirty-one years written the next thing, which was an ending.

Below her, at the foot of the tower, the sea slapped the landing steps. She heard it the way a person hears their own pulse, only when listening for it.

At eight, the boat came. It was not Declan's boat but a smaller, brighter one, and out of it climbed a young woman in a yellow jacket that had clearly never been rained on. She carried a tablet in a rubber case and she stood on the landing looking up at the tower with an expression Ysolde recognized, because she had worn it herself the first morning: the look of someone who has been told a place is small and is discovering that it is merely tall.

"Mrs. Marrick?" the woman called. "I'm Priya. From Harcourt Hospitality. Declan said it would be all right."

"Declan says a lot of things are all right," Ysolde said, but she came down the iron stair and took the line and made it fast, because you did not leave a stranger's boat swinging whatever you thought of her business.

Priya Venkataraghavan turned out to be an architect, not a salesperson, which Ysolde found harder to dislike. She had measurements to take. She had a laser device that she pointed at walls and which produced small red dots, and Ysolde followed her from the oil store to the lamp room, watching the dots slide across the whitewash like nervous insects.

"It's extraordinary," Priya said in the lamp room. The great Fresnel lens, retired in 2006 and replaced by a polycarbonate beacon the size of a bucket, still stood in its brass cradle, a beehive of glass prisms that threw the morning into a hundred small rainbows across the floor. "Do you know it weighs two tons? And it floats on a bath of mercury. Did you know that?"

"I polished it for twelve years before they took it out of service. I know exactly what it weighs, because I had to move the weights."

"Of course. Sorry." Priya lowered the laser. She was quiet for a moment and then said, in a different voice, "They're going to want to keep it. As a feature. In the lobby, or the bar. I'm told a lens like this is a selling point."

"A selling point," Ysolde said.

"I'm not going to pretend I'm sorry about the commission. It's a good project. But I grew up in Leeds, Mrs. Marrick. The sea was a rumor. I just want you to know I don't think a hotel is nothing." She said it quickly, as if she had rehearsed it on the boat and might lose her nerve.

Ysolde looked at her for long enough that the young woman shifted her feet. Then she took the green ledger from the shelf where she had left it and laid it open on the brass rail.

"Read that," she said. "Not all of it. The last week."

Priya read. Her lips moved slightly, which Ysolde liked. Wind NE force 7. Spray over the gallery at noon. Two hundred and six shearwaters on the south ledge, counted. A container ship, Hanjin something, standing too close; called the coastguard; they were polite and already knew. The light lit at 1709 by the gray box, and I watched it do it, and it did it well.

"You write about the box," Priya said, surprised.

"I write about everything the sea does. The box is part of what the sea does, now. That's the thing nobody in Declan's office understood when they made me non-operational. A lighthouse isn't a lamp. It's a person standing in the same place long enough for the place to learn her name."

"And the hotel?"

"The hotel will have a very nice lamp," Ysolde said, "and very good towels."

Priya laughed, and then, because laughing in that room felt faintly sacrilegious, stopped. She spent four hours measuring. Ysolde made her lunch, a crab sandwich, which the young woman ate standing up on the gallery with the wind snatching at her hair, saying nothing at all, which was the most intelligent thing she did all day.

When the boat left, the afternoon had gone pearl-colored and the long swell from the west had grown a little. Ysolde watched the yellow jacket shrink to a dot. Then she went back to the ledger, and under the day's entries, in the space Halvard Tegg had reserved for feelings and she had never filled, she wrote a single line.

"The place knows my name. It will have to learn another."

She closed the book, lit no lamp, and went down to the kitchen to start supper. Above her, at 1741, the gray box clicked, and the beam began its slow, indifferent, faithful turn across the water.

THE ORCHARD RECKONING

There is a particular silence that falls in an apple orchard after the last picker has gone home. It is not true silence; the wind goes on arguing with the leaves and there is always some small animal rearranging itself in the grass. But it is the silence of a place that has finished being useful for the day, and Thaddeus Boone had come to prefer it to the noise of the day's work, which had given him nothing but a bad knee and a ledger that refused to balance.

He sat on an upturned crate at the end of Row Nine with his boots off and counted bins in his head. Four hundred and twelve bins of Honeycrisp this season, against five hundred and ninety the year before. Frost in April, a hailstorm in July, and in August a man from the county who walked the property with a clipboard and the sorrowful politeness of a mortician. The bank did not care about frost.

"You're sulking in the good Cortlands," said a voice behind him.

His daughter Imogen stood at the end of the row with her arms folded, still in the canvas apron she wore for sorting. She was twenty-nine and had come home from Chicago in March ostensibly for two weeks. Seven months later, her apartment lease had lapsed and her houseplants had been given to a neighbor, and neither of them had mentioned it.

"I'm not sulking. I'm accounting."

"You're doing both. It's an old family talent." She sat down in the grass, uninvited, and plucked a windfall apple and turned it over. "This one's got a worm."

"Then eat around it."

"Dad."

"What?"

She put the apple down. For a long minute she said nothing, and he waited, because he had learned that Imogen, like the trees, would not be hurried and would punish him if he tried. Across the field a barn owl lifted off the roof of the cold store and went silently about its business.

"I got an email," she said finally. "From a company called Fennimore Growers. They do cider, mostly. Hard cider, a lot of it, for restaurants. They want to buy the fruit that doesn't make grade. The windfalls, the hail-marked ones, the ones the packers reject."

"We sell that to the juice plant."

"For nine cents a pound. They're offering twenty-two. And they'd sign a three-year contract, so the bank would see something steady." She looked at him sidelong. "I may have called them first."

Thaddeus said nothing. He was a man for whom change had always arrived as a letter from an institution, and it was strange to hear it arrive in his daughter's voice, under his own trees, smelling of cut grass and rot.

"Twenty-two cents," he said at last.

"Twenty-two."

"And what do they want from us besides apples?"

"That's the part I thought you'd hate." She took a breath. "They want us to stop spraying the lower blocks. Organic certification. It takes three years to convert, and the first two the yields drop. But the cider people pay a premium, and the premium covers it, mostly, if the winters cooperate."

"If the winters cooperate."

"I know."

The owl came back, quartering the grass at the edge of the orchard, hunting voles. Thaddeus watched it and thought about his own father, who had planted Row Nine in 1971 and who had believed, with the stubborn faith of a man who had survived a war and a lot of bad weather, that the only reliable thing on a farm was the next season. His father had sprayed whatever the extension office recommended and never once asked what it did to the owls.

"Your grandfather would have thrown you off the property."

"Maybe," Imogen said. "Or he'd have asked how many bins."

It was true, and it startled a laugh out of him, rough and rusty, the first in some weeks. He rubbed his bad knee and then, to his own surprise, held out a hand. She put the wormy apple in it. He turned it over, found the small neat hole, and bit into the other side.

"Sour," he said.

"It's a cider apple. They're supposed to be."

"Then maybe that's what the whole orchard has been trying to tell us." He chewed, considering. "Send them a reply. Tell them we'll talk. Tell them Thaddeus Boone wants to see the contract before he sees a lawyer, and he wants it in English."

"Dad, I already told them that."

"Of course you did." He shook his head slowly, and for the first time in months the ledger in his mind stopped refusing him. "Get the other crate. I'm not sitting here by myself."

She fetched it. The two of them sat in the dusk in Row Nine, a farmer and his daughter and a worm-eaten apple, while the owl worked the field and the first cold stars came out over the Cortlands.

AFTERIMAGE

The transit authority on Kepler Station had a rule that no ship carrying the dead could dock on a public berth, and so Commander Idris Okonkwo-Reyes brought the Lantern Fish in through the freight lock at the hour when the shift changed and the corridors were crowded enough that nobody looked up.

She had practice at not being looked at. Eleven years in survey service teaches you to move through a crowd like a fish through reeds. But the cargo she carried was not the kind you could hide behind a crowd, and by the time she reached Dock Nine she could feel the small hairs rise on her arms at every glance.

The cargo was one man. His name was Ansel Verhoeven, he had been a xenobiologist, and he had been dead for fourteen months, though the thing in the cryo-cradle behind her was not exactly a corpse. Seven hundred and twenty days earlier, on the ice moon Tethys-Minor, Verhoeven had walked into a cavern of luminous fungal stalks and had come out three hours later speaking in a voice that was not entirely his own. He had gone on speaking for a week. Then he had stopped, and the doctors on the survey ship had pronounced a cause of death that was a polite way of saying they did not know.

Idris had loved him, in the careful, unspoken way that people on long deployments love. And in the last hour of his life he had grasped her wrist and said, quite clearly and in his own voice, "Take me to Halloran. Tell her it's in the afterimage."

Dr. Maren Halloran received them in a laboratory so clean it had the faint smell of nothing, the way a cold room smells of cold. She was small and very upright and wore her gray hair cropped close, and she listened to Idris's account without once looking at the cradle, which Idris found she respected.

"The afterimage," Halloran said when Idris had finished. "He said it like that? That exact word?"

"Yes."

"Then I think I know what he meant. Sit down, Commander. This will take a little time, and you won't like the shape of it."

She brought up a display on the wall, a slow three-dimensional map of branching filaments, and Idris recognized the structure from the fungal cavern: the same lattice, the same pale, pulsing glow.

"You know that when you stare at a bright light and then close your eyes, you continue to see it. A ghost of the thing, printed on the retina. We call that an afterimage. What I believe the organism on Tethys-Minor does is something similar, but at the level of neural tissue. It doesn't communicate in the sense we mean. It imprints. A mind passes through, and the organism records a pattern of that mind's firing, as faithfully as a photograph, and then it plays the pattern back through whatever nervous system is nearest."

"So the voice."

"Wasn't Ansel. It was an echo of something that had passed through that cavern long before he did. Maybe thousands of years before." Halloran's voice was level, but her hands, folded in her lap, had tightened. "And then the pattern burned itself into him. His own mind started to give way to it, the way an old film gets overwritten by a new exposure."

Idris looked at the cradle for the first time since entering. The frost on its lid had begun to bead. "He said it's in the afterimage. That means there's something still there. Of him."

"That's my suspicion. If the organism recorded him as it recorded the others, there will be a residue in the tissue. Not a person. I want to be very clear about that. A pattern, a set of habits of firing. But enough, perhaps, to read."

"To read." Idris tasted the word. "And then what? You talk to it?"

"I don't know. I don't know whether what we'd find would feel anything, or whether it would be only an elaborate recording, like a letter that keeps saying the same sentence. That is the part you won't like. We would be asking a machine, essentially, to tell us whether it is a man."

The room hummed. Somewhere in the walls, a pump cycled. Idris thought of Ansel on the observation deck of the survey ship, three weeks into the deployment, arguing with her about whether the moons of Tethys were old or merely patient. He had been wrong, and delighted to be wrong, and she had thought, I will remember this forever, which was what everyone thinks and no one does.

"Do it," she said.

"Commander, you should consider—"

"I considered for fourteen months, Doctor. I did the considering at three in the morning on a ship with a body in the hold. I've run out of considering." She stood. "He asked me to bring him here. I brought him. Whatever you find, I want to be in the room."

Halloran studied her for a long moment, then nodded once, a small, formal gesture, as between two people sealing a contract.

It took nine hours. At the end of it the wall display flickered and a single line of text appeared, in the flat, unpunctuated cadence of a decoded signal, and Idris, who had braced for strangeness, found instead that it was almost unbearably ordinary.

it was cold in the cavern and I thought of you and the argument about the moons

She said nothing for a long time. Halloran, wisely, said nothing either. At length Idris reached out and laid her palm against the cold glass of the cradle, and spoke very softly, the way one speaks to a sleeper.

"Patient," she said. "You were right. They were patient."

The text on the wall did not change. It was only a recording, after all, and recordings cannot answer. But Idris stood there with her hand on the glass until the lights cycled down for the night watch, and when she finally left, she was surprised to find that she was not weeping, only lighter, as if some long-carried weight had finally been set on the ground and found to be the exact size of the hands that had held it.

THE CASE OF THE MISSING METRONOME

Inspector Hollis Tamworth-Lyle disliked cases that began with a theft of something nobody could possibly want. A diamond, yes; a diamond had a market. But a metronome?

"A Maelzel, mind you," said Mrs. Cordelia Pettigrew, who had called the constabulary at seven that morning and was now conducting the Inspector through the music room of Ashby House with the brisk proprietary air of a woman showing off a prize heifer. "Walnut case, brass weight, wound by key. Belonged to my late husband's grandfather. Valued at, oh, perhaps two hundred pounds, but of course to us it's priceless."

"Naturally," said Tamworth-Lyle, who had learned that this was a safe thing to say in almost any circumstance. "And when did you last see it?"

"Last night. Mr. Fenwick, my nephew, was practicing the Schumann. He always uses it. He is quite devoted. He put it on the piano at ten and the house was locked by eleven. This morning, gone."

The room was handsome and cold. A Broadwood grand stood under the window with its lid up. There was a pale rectangle on the polished top, a clean oblong in the thin layer of dust, where the metronome had sat. The Inspector bent, squinting. He did not touch it.

"The windows?"

"Latched. The doors locked, and I keep the keys."

"And who is in the house?"

"Myself. My nephew Fenwick. Mrs. Dunmore the cook, who sleeps in. The maid, Tilly. And Dr. Abelard Quince, my husband's physician, who has been staying while he recovers from a chest complaint." She ticked them off on her gloved fingers like items on a shopping list. "Five of us. Six, counting you."

"I would rather not be counted, madam, if it is all the same."

He interviewed them in the morning room one at a time. Fenwick Pettigrew, a pale young man with ink on his cuffs, was indignant. "Of course I didn't take it! I'd sooner steal my own hand. Without the metronome I've no idea whether I'm rushing the second movement. I rush, you see. Dreadfully." Mrs. Dunmore the cook was stout and unflappable and said she had been in the kitchen from five and would have noticed a burglar because the kitchen cat noticed everything. Tilly, the maid, was seventeen and trembling and said she hadn't been in the music room since Tuesday, not once, and there was no use asking.

"I haven't asked, Tilly," the Inspector said gently.

"No, sir. Sorry, sir."

That was the first oddity. People who have nothing to hide seldom volunteer an alibi nobody has requested. But Tamworth-Lyle filed it away and moved to the physician, who was sitting by the fire in a plaid rug, wheezing slightly, with a sheaf of medical journals open on his knees.

"A metronome," said Dr. Quince, in the tone of a man pronouncing a rare disease. "Extraordinary. Who would steal a metronome?"

"That is rather the question."

"I should think someone with an ear for rhythm and no scruples." He chuckled and coughed. "I am joking, Inspector. I was in bed at ten. I took my powder, as I do every night, and slept like the dead."

"The powder. What is it?"

"Veronal. Doctor's orders. Physician, heal thyself and so on." He smiled. "You'll find the bottle on my nightstand."

Tamworth-Lyle thanked him and returned to the music room alone. He stood for some time looking at the clean oblong in the dust. Then he did what he often did when baffled: he stopped looking at what was missing and looked instead at what was there.

On the piano's music desk lay the Schumann, open to the second movement. Over the page, in pencil, someone had marked a series of small tick marks above the bar lines, in groups of four. Nothing unusual in that. A pianist counting. But in the margin beside the final bars, in a different pencil and a different hand, a figure had been scribbled and half erased: 6 5 / 4 3 / 4.

He frowned at it. It was not a tempo marking. A tempo would read a hundred and something. It looked more like a series of numbers somebody had noted in haste.

He crouched and looked under the piano. Nothing. He ran a hand along the lower edge of the instrument's case and his finger caught on a tiny scrap of paper wedged beneath the pedal lyre. He drew it out with tweezers from his waistcoat pocket. It was a ticket, small and gray: a left-luggage receipt from the railway station at Ludlow, stamped with yesterday's date.

"Mrs. Pettigrew," he said, returning to the morning room, "does your nephew travel to Ludlow?"

"Never. Fenwick hates trains."

"Does anyone in the household?"

There was a pause that stretched slightly too long. Then Tilly, standing by the door with a tray, dropped a teacup.

It took very little after that. The girl wept and the story came out in tatters: her brother Albert, a railway porter at Ludlow, who had a debt to a bookmaker; a conversation overheard in the kitchen about the Maelzel's worth; a plan, foolish, hasty, ill-considered, to take it to the left-luggage office and let Albert sell it on. She had done it at midnight with the spare key from Mrs. Pettigrew's drawer, on her bare feet, terrified. She had dropped the ticket accidentally in the dark.

"And the numbers in the margin of the Schumann?" the Inspector asked.

"I don't know anything about numbers, sir. Honestly."

He believed her. And that, he reflected later, was the puzzle's last small joy. The numbers belonged to Fenwick, who admitted, blushing, that they were the tempo markings he had been trying out through the night: sixty-five, then sixty-four, then a hundred and forty-four. He had been experimenting, and he had indeed been rushing.

The Inspector recovered the metronome from the Ludlow left-luggage office that afternoon. It was ticking quietly in its walnut case, sitting on a shelf between a hatbox and a dead man's umbrella, set, by some accident of the porter's curiosity, to a gentle andante. Mrs. Pettigrew declined to press charges, on the condition that Tilly be given a more suitable situation, and Fenwick played the second movement of the Schumann that evening with the Maelzel at his elbow, slowly, correctly, and for the first time in recorded history not rushing at all.

TWO LINES AT THE EDGE OF THE DESERT

"You can't park here."

"I can park anywhere I like. It's a desert."

"It's a protected wash. There's a sign."

"The sign says 'Flash flood area.' There hasn't been a flood here since 1987. I looked it up." The woman in the van leaned out the window. She had sunburned shoulders, a cracked phone propped on the dashboard, and the look of someone who has been awake for a long time on principle. "Are you a ranger?"

"Volunteer. Reyes. I patrol the Nine Mile." The man in the faded green vest hooked his thumbs through his belt. He was somewhere past sixty, with a gray mustache like a tidy broom. "You're going to be trapped if it rains."

"It's not going to rain."

"It's July. It's the monsoon."

"The forecast says five percent."

"The forecast," Reyes said with feeling, "is written by a man in an air-conditioned office in Phoenix who has never in his life stood in a wash and listened to the ground."

She laughed in spite of herself. It was a short, surprised, honest laugh, and he decided he liked her slightly, which complicated his plan to be stern.

"Listen to the ground," she said. "What does that mean?"

"It means that about a half hour before the water comes there's a sound. Like a freight train a long way off. If you're lucky you hear it."

"And if you're not?"

"Then you're a news story."

She looked at the sky. It was a hard flat blue without a blemish on it. Then she looked back at him, and something in her face gave a little.

"My name is Dolores," she said. "I've been driving for nine days. I was supposed to be in Santa Fe to see my sister. She's sick. I mean she's not dying, but she's, you know." She waved a hand vaguely. "I stopped because I couldn't keep my eyes open, and there was nobody, and it was quiet. I'm sorry. I'll move."

"You don't have to apologize."

"I don't like being told what to do by strangers."

"I don't like telling anybody what to do. It's the worst part of the job." He glanced at the horizon, where a faint smudge of cloud, no larger than a thumbprint, sat low over the ridgeline. He said nothing about it. "Tell you what. There's a pullout up the road, a quarter mile, on high ground. Nobody'll bother you there. And I've got a thermos of coffee that's worse than any you've ever had."

"Is that an offer?"

"It's a warning."

Dolores started the van. In the rearview mirror the old man's figure got smaller and smaller, standing in the road with his thermos raised like a lantern, and behind him the little cloud on the ridge had begun, very slowly, to grow.

They sat at the pullout until dark, and the coffee was indeed terrible. At a quarter to nine they heard, far off, a sound like a freight train, and neither of them said anything. Then, below them, in the dark where the van had been parked, the wash filled with a low brown roar, carrying tumbleweeds and a broken fencepost and the whole astonished silence of the desert downstream.

"Huh," said Dolores.

"Yep," said Reyes.

She stayed for a second cup. In the morning, before she left, she wrote her sister's number on a napkin and gave it to him, and he gave her the thermos, and neither of them could have said why.

SCENE: THE BOARDROOM

The conference room on the fortieth floor had a view of the river and a table that seated twenty. Eight people sat at it, which made it feel emptier than it was.

"So let me see if I understand," said Gwendolyn Achterberg, the chair, tapping a pen against her tablet. "We're proposing to acquire a company that's losing eleven million a quarter, in order to save a division that's losing twelve."

"When you put it that way," said the CFO.

"Is there another way to put it?"

"There's the way where we call it a strategic realignment," said Boaz Lindqvist, from operations, and a nervous ripple went around the table.

"Boaz."

"Sorry. Yes. I'll withdraw that."

Gwendolyn set the pen down. She was sixty-three, had run the company for fourteen years, and had a reputation for asking exactly one question at each meeting that ended up being the only one that mattered. The room waited for it.

"Who," she said, "on this side of the table has actually used their product?"

Silence. A chair creaked.

"I have," said a voice from the far end, and eight heads turned. It was Nkechi, the youngest person in the room, a product analyst who had been invited only to carry the slides and who had gone quite pale. "I used it for about three weeks. In my own time. I wanted to see how the onboarding worked."

"And?"

"It's good." She swallowed. "It's really good, actually. The numbers are bad because their sales team is terrible, not because the software is. If you took the product and put it in our channel, I think customers would—" She stopped. "Sorry. It's not my place."

"It is entirely your place. You are the only person here who has used the thing." Gwendolyn turned to the CFO, an unlucky man named Pruitt. "Walt, I'd like a revised model by Friday. Assume we keep the product, drop their sales organization, and retain Ms. Okafor here as an adviser to the integration. Full salary and title. Can you do that?"

"Madam Chair, with respect, a product analyst on an integration team is—"

"Walt."

"Yes. Friday. Of course."

Nkechi sat very still. Under the table her hands were shaking. She had gone into the meeting expecting to carry slides and she was coming out of it with a career, and the sensation was exactly like stepping onto a stair that is not there.

"One more thing," said Gwendolyn, gathering her tablet. "Everyone who votes yes on this deal should use the product for one week. Not read about it. Use it. If you can't tell me what it feels like to be their customer, you have no business buying their company."

She left. The room breathed out. And Boaz Lindqvist leaned across the table toward Nkechi and whispered, with something like reverence, "You do know she's never said that to anybody before."

"Said what?"

"Your name," he said. "She remembered your name."

WHAT THE RIVER KEPT

My grandmother never learned to swim, and she lived beside the river for eighty-one years. When I asked her about this, the summer I was eleven, she looked at me over the rim of her enamel cup as if I had asked why she had never learned to fly.

"You do not swim in a thing you are in conversation with," she said.

The river was the Vesna, which in her village meant spring, though it was a brown, slow, thoughtful river that seemed to have given up on the season a long time ago. It ran past the back of her garden in a wide bend, and in the evenings the light came down through the willows and turned the water the color of weak tea. She had a bench there, a plank on two stumps, and she sat on it every evening with her cup and her silence and her particular way of tilting her head, as though listening for a footfall.

I was sent to her that year because my parents were not speaking to each other in the way that parents do before they stop speaking altogether. No one explained this. I was given a duffel bag, a train ticket and a stern instruction to be useful, and I arrived in a village of forty houses with the vague conviction that I had been exiled for something I had done.

My grandmother did not ask what I had done. She handed me a bucket.

The first week was all buckets. I carried water to the beans, I carried scraps to the hens, I carried a bucket of ash from the stove to the compost with such gravity that she gently took it from me and said, "It is ash, Tomas. It will not bite." In the afternoons, when the heat made the whole valley drowsy, she taught me the names of things: the heron that stood in the shallows like a bent nail, the kingfisher that was a flash of blue and nothing else, the willow-herb that grew in the ditches and turned pink in August.

On the ninth day I found the box.

It was in the crawl space under the kitchen, where I had gone looking for a lost hen. An old tin biscuit box, rusted at the hinges, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a string that had gone stiff as wire. Inside were seven stones. They were river stones, smooth and gray, each about the size of a plum, and each had a name scratched into it with a nail. I recognized none of them. Pavel. Irena. Little Josef. Old Marta. Anton. Stefa. And one that had no name at all, only a small crooked cross.

I carried the box up into the light and set it on the kitchen table, and my grandmother, who was peeling potatoes, did not look surprised. She wiped her hands on her apron and sat down across from me.

"So," she said. "You found the river's share."

She told me the story slowly, in pieces, the way she told everything. When she was a girl, the Vesna flooded every spring, and the village lost something to it each year: a goat, a fence, a boat. Her own grandmother had begun the custom. Whenever the river took someone, a person, not a goat, the family went down to the bank at the lowest water of the following autumn and chose a stone, and wrote the name on it, and kept it. "So that the river would not be the only one remembering," she said.

"But why do you have them?"

"Because nobody else wanted them." She said it without bitterness. "People want to forget. They put them away, they say that is enough. But a name is like a seed. If you bury it, it comes up somewhere else, in some way you cannot predict. It is better to hold it where you can see it."

"Who's the one with no name?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside a hen was complaining and the heat pressed at the window glass.

"That one is mine," she said. "I carved the cross but I never knew what to put under it. He was a baby, Tomas. Not born long enough to be given a name. My first. The river took him in the flood of fifty-four, when the water came into the house in the night, and I was nineteen and I held him above my head until the roof gave way, and then I did not have him anymore."

I did not know what to say, and so for once I said nothing. She reached out and put her dry, strong hand on top of mine and left it there.

"This is why I do not swim," she said, very gently. "Do you understand? I do not trust myself in that water. Not because I am afraid. Because I would not come out again."

That evening we went down to the bench together. The light was coming through the willows, and the heron stood where it always stood, a bent nail against the brown, and for a while neither of us spoke. When it got dark enough that the first bats appeared, my grandmother said, "Choose a stone."

I looked at her. "For what?"

"For whatever you are carrying. You came here carrying something. I can see it the way I can see the weather on the hills. You do not have to tell me what it is. But you should put a name on it."

I went down to the water's edge, to the shallows where the pebbles gleamed in the last gray light, and picked one that fit in my palm. Then I walked back up the bank and she gave me the nail, and I sat there with it for a very long time, trying to decide what to write.

In the end I wrote only one word. It was not the name of a person. It was the name of the quiet kitchen in the apartment where my parents had stopped talking, and the dish in the sink, and the radio turned low. I wrote home, and then, underneath it, because it seemed to belong, I scratched a small crooked cross.

My grandmother nodded as if I had said something wise. "Good," she said. "Now it is in the box. And now it is something that has happened, and not something that is happening."

I have thought about that sentence for forty years. I did not understand it then. My parents divorced the following winter, and I did not weep, and I was angry at myself for not weeping, until the spring when I finally did, on a train, in front of a very surprised conductor. But in my wallet I have carried ever since a photograph of seven river stones on an oilcloth, and, behind it, a small flat gray pebble that I took from the Vesna before I left. It has no name on it. It does not need one. It is only something I carry, which is a different thing, I have learned, from something that carries me.

THE FOURTH ELEVATOR

Overheard in the lobby of the Meridian Tower, 5:47 p.m., Friday:

"Is that one broken?"

"That's the fourth elevator."

"I know it's the fourth elevator. There are only four. I'm asking if it's broken."

"It's not broken. It's, you know. The fourth elevator."

The first speaker, a lean man in a gray suit and a lanyard that said CONTRACTOR in blunt capitals, looked at the second, who was holding a paper coffee cup and wearing a security guard's blazer with the name tag KENNETH. Behind Kenneth the lobby stretched in marble and chrome, nearly empty at this hour. Three elevator doors stood open with the patient look of mouths about to speak. The fourth was closed, with a small laminated sign taped to the center: NOT IN SERVICE.

"So it's broken."

"Technically it's not in service."

"What's the difference?"

Kenneth sipped. He had the air of a man who had been asked this before and had once answered it, and had not enjoyed the consequences. "Broken means somebody has to fix it. Not in service means somebody decided."

"Decided what?"

"That it's not in service."

The contractor, whose name was Arjun Bhattacharya-Lowe and who had spent eleven hours wiring a data closet on the nineteenth floor, rubbed his eyes. "Kenneth. I want to go home. I have a toddler and a cat and a leak in my bathroom. I'm not looking for a philosophical discussion. Which elevators go to the garage?"

"One, two, and three."

"Then why would I take the fourth?"

"You wouldn't," said Kenneth. "That's the thing. Nobody does. It's been out since October. Management put the sign up, and then they were going to fix it, and then there was a budget thing, and then it just sort of became part of the building. Like a coat rack."

"A coat rack."

"A very large coat rack that goes up and down." Kenneth shrugged. "Sometimes at night, though, I hear it."

"Hear it."

"Going up. Past the twelfth floor, it always stops at twelve. Then I hear the doors. Then nothing. And then about forty minutes later it comes back down."

Arjun regarded him for a long moment. In the deserted lobby the air conditioning ticked. "You're telling me there's a ghost in the not-in-service elevator."

"I'm not telling you anything. I'm a security guard. I'm saying what I hear."

"Have you checked?"

"Checked what?"

"The twelfth floor. When it stops."

"I'm not paid to go to twelve, sir." Kenneth finished his coffee and crumpled the cup. "Twelve is where the accounting firm used to be, before they left. There's nothing up there. Just the carpet and the water cooler. And, to be honest, I'd like to keep it that way."

"Kenneth, you've been working this building for how long?"

"Nineteen years."

"And you've never gone up there."

"I've gone up there a hundred times, sir. Not at night. Not after the fourth elevator."

For a moment Arjun considered pressing the matter. But he was tired, and it was Friday, and the toddler was waiting. He picked up his bag and walked to elevator two, and as the doors began to close he looked back and saw the guard standing in the middle of the lobby, staring at the laminated sign with an expression that was neither fear nor longing but something quieter, the look of a man who has been keeping a secret so long it has become a friend.

On the way down, Arjun found himself humming. It took him until the garage to realize it was a hymn.

THE LAST BUS TO WENDOVER

The bus was late, which on the Wendover line was less an event than a climate. Odalys stood under the plastic shelter with her suitcase between her feet and a paper bag of chestnuts going cold in her hand, and across the road a man in an orange coat was also waiting, and neither of them was willing to admit it.

It was the last bus. Everyone knew that. After it came nothing until six the next morning, and the nearest hotel was fourteen miles away in a town whose name she had never learned to pronounce. So she waited, and the man waited, and the wind came down off the fields carrying the smell of wet earth and distant woodsmoke.

"You can share the shelter," she called at last. "It's got a roof. Mostly."

He crossed the road with the unhurried gait of a man who had never been in a hurry in his life and stood at the opposite end of the bench. He was perhaps seventy. He carried a violin case, battered, held together at one corner with silver tape.

"Chestnut?" Odalys offered.

"Thank you. I haven't had one in years." He cracked it with his thumbnail and ate it with solemn attention. "My wife used to roast them on the stove. She burned half of them. I preferred the burned ones."

"Used to."

"Yes." He nodded. "Eleven months in March."

She did not say she was sorry, because she was tired of hearing the word herself. Instead she asked, "What do you play?"

"Whatever's asked. Weddings, mostly. Funerals, when there's no organist. Once a very strange birthday for a parrot." He tapped the case. "Tonight there was a christening in Ashgrove. The baby slept through the whole of the Bach. A critic, you see. Unimpressed."

She smiled. The wind picked up and she pulled her scarf closer, and he looked at it, a green wool thing with a loose thread at one end, and said, "That is a good scarf."

"My sister knitted it. She's terrible at it. This is the fourth attempt." Odalys turned the loose thread between her fingers. "I'm going to her now. Wendover. She just had twins and she says she needs somebody to hold things. I think she means her sanity."

"Twins," said the old man. "That is a great deal of crying."

"Is it?"

"I raised three. I recommend earplugs and a very patient dog."

They fell silent. A single headlight appeared far down the road, wavered, and turned out to be a tractor. Odalys felt a small absurd pang. She had counted on the bus the way you count on a ceiling.

"Do you know," the violinist said after a while, "I think I would like to play something."

"Here?"

"Why not? There is no one to disturb. Only a hedgehog or two."

He opened the case with the careful, tender hands of a man handling something asleep. The violin was old and dark and slightly scarred, and when he tucked it under his chin the shelter seemed to grow smaller and warmer. He tuned for a moment, a few long, aching fifths, and then he played.

It was not anything she knew. It was slow and plain, a little like a lullaby, a little like a song somebody sings alone in a kitchen. Odalys closed her eyes. The wind seemed to hold its breath. Across the dark fields a single window went gold in a farmhouse, and she thought, absurdly, that it was listening.

When he finished, neither of them spoke.

"What was it?" she said finally.

"It does not have a name. My wife and I made it up on our honeymoon, in a rented room in Lisbon with a leaking ceiling. I have played it every night since March." He lowered the violin. "I have not played it for anyone before."

The bus arrived four minutes later, its headlights sweeping the shelter like a lighthouse, its doors sighing open with a hydraulic complaint. The driver, a woman in a knitted hat, looked at the two of them without comment.

"Wendover?" said Odalys.

"And Ashgrove after," said the driver. "Hop in, both of you. It's freezing."

They rode in the back, the violin case between them on the seat, and neither of them said much of anything. At Ashgrove the old man rose, and nodded, and for a moment Odalys thought he would say something large. Instead he only touched the brim of an imaginary hat.

"Hold the twins," he said. "Everything else can wait."

Then the doors closed, and the bus pulled out into the dark, and she watched him walk up a gravel lane toward a lit window, small and upright in his orange coat, until the road bent and he was gone.

"""Data models for a small warehouse inventory tracker.

The models are deliberately plain dataclasses so they can be serialized to
JSON without any extra machinery. Quantities are integers (units on hand);
prices are stored in integer cents to avoid floating point surprises.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import datetime as dt
import json
from dataclasses import dataclass, field, asdict
from typing import Dict, Iterable, List, Optional


class InventoryError(Exception):
    """Base class for all errors raised by this package."""


class InsufficientStock(InventoryError):
    def __init__(self, sku: str, wanted: int, available: int) -> None:
        super().__init__(
            f"cannot remove {wanted} of {sku}: only {available} on hand"
        )
        self.sku = sku
        self.wanted = wanted
        self.available = available


@dataclass
class Item:
    sku: str
    name: str
    price_cents: int
    quantity: int = 0
    reorder_level: int = 5
    tags: List[str] = field(default_factory=list)

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        if self.price_cents < 0:
            raise ValueError("price_cents must be non-negative")
        if self.quantity < 0:
            raise ValueError("quantity must be non-negative")
        self.sku = self.sku.strip().upper()

    @property
    def price(self) -> float:
        return self.price_cents / 100.0

    @property
    def needs_reorder(self) -> bool:
        return self.quantity <= self.reorder_level

    def total_value_cents(self) -> int:
        return self.price_cents * self.quantity


@dataclass
class Movement:
    """A single change to stock levels, kept for auditing."""

    sku: str
    delta: int
    reason: str
    timestamp: dt.datetime = field(default_factory=dt.datetime.utcnow)

    def to_dict(self) -> dict:
        d = asdict(self)
        d["timestamp"] = self.timestamp.isoformat(timespec="seconds")
        return d


class Inventory:
    """In-memory collection of items with an append-only movement log."""

    def __init__(self, items: Optional[Iterable[Item]] = None) -> None:
        self._items: Dict[str, Item] = {}
        self._log: List[Movement] = []
        for item in items or ():
            self.add_item(item)

    # -- basic access -------------------------------------------------------

    def __len__(self) -> int:
        return len(self._items)

    def __contains__(self, sku: str) -> bool:
        return sku.strip().upper() in self._items

    def get(self, sku: str) -> Item:
        try:
            return self._items[sku.strip().upper()]
        except KeyError:
            raise InventoryError(f"unknown sku: {sku!r}") from None

    def add_item(self, item: Item) -> None:
        if item.sku in self._items:
            raise InventoryError(f"duplicate sku: {item.sku}")
        self._items[item.sku] = item
        if item.quantity:
            self._log.append(Movement(item.sku, item.quantity, "initial"))

    # -- stock changes --------------------------------------------------------

    def receive(self, sku: str, count: int, reason: str = "receipt") -> int:
        if count <= 0:
            raise ValueError("count must be positive")
        item = self.get(sku)
        item.quantity += count
        self._log.append(Movement(item.sku, count, reason))
        return item.quantity

    def remove(self, sku: str, count: int, reason: str = "shipment") -> int:
        if count <= 0:
            raise ValueError("count must be positive")
        item = self.get(sku)
        if item.quantity < count:
            raise InsufficientStock(item.sku, count, item.quantity)
        item.quantity -= count
        self._log.append(Movement(item.sku, -count, reason))
        return item.quantity

    # -- reporting ------------------------------------------------------------

    def low_stock(self) -> List[Item]:
        return sorted(
            (i for i in self._items.values() if i.needs_reorder),
            key=lambda i: (i.quantity - i.reorder_level, i.sku),
        )

    def total_value_cents(self) -> int:
        return sum(i.total_value_cents() for i in self._items.values())

    def by_tag(self, tag: str) -> List[Item]:
        return [i for i in self._items.values() if tag in i.tags]

    def history(self, sku: Optional[str] = None) -> List[Movement]:
        if sku is None:
            return list(self._log)
        key = sku.strip().upper()
        return [m for m in self._log if m.sku == key]

    # -- persistence ----------------------------------------------------------

    def to_json(self) -> str:
        payload = {
            "items": [asdict(i) for i in self._items.values()],
            "log": [m.to_dict() for m in self._log],
        }
        return json.dumps(payload, indent=2, sort_keys=True)

    @classmethod
    def from_json(cls, text: str) -> "Inventory":
        data = json.loads(text)
        inv = cls()
        for raw in data.get("items", []):
            inv._items[raw["sku"]] = Item(**raw)
        for raw in data.get("log", []):
            ts = dt.datetime.fromisoformat(raw.pop("timestamp"))
            inv._log.append(Movement(timestamp=ts, **raw))
        return inv

"""Command line front end.

Usage examples::

    python -m inventory.cli --file stock.json list
    python -m inventory.cli --file stock.json receive WIDGET-1 40
    python -m inventory.cli --file stock.json low --format csv
"""

import argparse
import csv
import sys
from pathlib import Path
from typing import List, Optional

from .models import Inventory, InventoryError, Item


def load(path: Path) -> Inventory:
    if not path.exists():
        return Inventory()
    return Inventory.from_json(path.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))


def save(inv: Inventory, path: Path) -> None:
    tmp = path.with_suffix(path.suffix + ".tmp")
    tmp.write_text(inv.to_json(), encoding="utf-8")
    tmp.replace(path)  # atomic on POSIX


def format_money(cents: int) -> str:
    sign = "-" if cents < 0 else ""
    cents = abs(cents)
    return f"{sign}${cents // 100:,}.{cents % 100:02d}"


def print_table(items: List[Item], out=sys.stdout) -> None:
    if not items:
        print("(no items)", file=out)
        return
    width = max(len(i.name) for i in items)
    print(f"{'SKU':<12} {'NAME':<{width}} {'QTY':>6} {'PRICE':>10}", file=out)
    for i in items:
        print(
            f"{i.sku:<12} {i.name:<{width}} {i.quantity:>6} "
            f"{format_money(i.price_cents):>10}",
            file=out,
        )


def print_csv(items: List[Item], out=sys.stdout) -> None:
    writer = csv.writer(out)
    writer.writerow(["sku", "name", "quantity", "price_cents"])
    for i in items:
        writer.writerow([i.sku, i.name, i.quantity, i.price_cents])


def build_parser() -> argparse.ArgumentParser:
    p = argparse.ArgumentParser(prog="inventory", description=__doc__.split("\n")[0])
    p.add_argument("--file", type=Path, default=Path("stock.json"),
                   help="path to the JSON data file (default: %(default)s)")
    sub = p.add_subparsers(dest="command", required=True)

    sub.add_parser("list", help="show every item")

    add = sub.add_parser("add", help="create a new item")
    add.add_argument("sku")
    add.add_argument("name")
    add.add_argument("price", type=float, help="unit price in dollars")
    add.add_argument("--qty", type=int, default=0)
    add.add_argument("--reorder", type=int, default=5)

    rec = sub.add_parser("receive", help="add stock")
    rec.add_argument("sku")
    rec.add_argument("count", type=int)

    ship = sub.add_parser("ship", help="remove stock")
    ship.add_argument("sku")
    ship.add_argument("count", type=int)

    low = sub.add_parser("low", help="items at or below reorder level")
    low.add_argument("--format", choices=("table", "csv"), default="table")

    sub.add_parser("value", help="total value of stock on hand")
    return p


def main(argv: Optional[List[str]] = None) -> int:
    args = build_parser().parse_args(argv)
    inv = load(args.file)
    try:
        if args.command == "list":
            print_table(sorted(inv._items.values(), key=lambda i: i.sku))
        elif args.command == "add":
            cents = round(args.price * 100)
            inv.add_item(Item(args.sku, args.name, cents, args.qty, args.reorder))
            save(inv, args.file)
        elif args.command == "receive":
            new_qty = inv.receive(args.sku, args.count)
            print(f"{args.sku.upper()}: now {new_qty}")
            save(inv, args.file)
        elif args.command == "ship":
            new_qty = inv.remove(args.sku, args.count)
            print(f"{args.sku.upper()}: now {new_qty}")
            save(inv, args.file)
        elif args.command == "low":
            items = inv.low_stock()
            (print_csv if args.format == "csv" else print_table)(items)
        elif args.command == "value":
            print(format_money(inv.total_value_cents()))
    except InventoryError as exc:
        print(f"error: {exc}", file=sys.stderr)
        return 2
    return 0


if __name__ == "__main__":
    raise SystemExit(main())

"""Rolling statistics over a stream of numbers, with no third party deps."""

import math
from collections import deque
from typing import Deque, Iterable, Iterator, Tuple


class RollingStats:
    """Maintain mean and variance over the last ``window`` samples.

    Uses Welford-style updates on both insertion and eviction so the cost per
    sample is O(1) and the result stays numerically stable for long streams.
    """

    def __init__(self, window: int) -> None:
        if window < 2:
            raise ValueError("window must be at least 2")
        self.window = window
        self._buf: Deque[float] = deque()
        self._mean = 0.0
        self._m2 = 0.0

    def push(self, x: float) -> None:
        if len(self._buf) == self.window:
            self._evict()
        self._buf.append(x)
        n = len(self._buf)
        delta = x - self._mean
        self._mean += delta / n
        self._m2 += delta * (x - self._mean)

    def _evict(self) -> None:
        old = self._buf.popleft()
        n = len(self._buf)  # size after removal
        if n == 0:
            self._mean = 0.0
            self._m2 = 0.0
            return
        delta = old - self._mean
        self._mean = (self._mean * (n + 1) - old) / n
        self._m2 -= delta * (old - self._mean)
        if self._m2 < 0.0:  # guard against tiny negative drift
            self._m2 = 0.0

    @property
    def mean(self) -> float:
        return self._mean

    @property
    def variance(self) -> float:
        n = len(self._buf)
        return self._m2 / (n - 1) if n > 1 else 0.0

    @property
    def stdev(self) -> float:
        return math.sqrt(self.variance)


def zscores(values: Iterable[float], window: int = 20) -> Iterator[Tuple[float, float]]:
    """Yield ``(value, z)`` where z is measured against the *previous* window."""
    stats = RollingStats(window)
    for i, v in enumerate(values):
        if i >= window and stats.stdev > 0:
            yield v, (v - stats.mean) / stats.stdev
        else:
            yield v, 0.0
        stats.push(v)


def detect_spikes(values, window=20, threshold=3.5):
    """Return indices whose z-score exceeds ``threshold`` in magnitude."""
    return [i for i, (_, z) in enumerate(zscores(values, window)) if abs(z) > threshold]

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Hit:
    ip: str
    when: datetime
    method: str
    path: str
    status: int
    size: int


def parse_line(line: str) -> Hit | None:
    m = LINE_RE.match(line)
    if not m:
        return None
    size = 0 if m["size"] == "-" else int(m["size"])
    when = datetime.strptime(m["ts"], "%d/%b/%Y:%H:%M:%S %z")
    return Hit(m["ip"], when, m["method"], m["path"], int(m["status"]), size)


def read_hits(stream: TextIO) -> Iterator[Hit]:
    bad = 0
    for line in stream:
        hit = parse_line(line)
        if hit is None:
            bad += 1
            continue
        yield hit
    if bad:
        print(f"skipped {bad} malformed lines", file=sys.stderr)


def summarize(hits: Iterator[Hit], top: int = 5) -> Dict[str, object]:
    by_status: Counter = Counter()
    by_path: Counter = Counter()
    bytes_by_ip: Dict[str, int] = defaultdict(int)
    hourly: Counter = Counter()
    total = 0
    for h in hits:
        total += 1
        by_status[h.status // 100 * 100] += 1
        by_path[h.path] += 1
        bytes_by_ip[h.ip] += h.size
        hourly[h.when.hour] += 1
    heavy: List = sorted(bytes_by_ip.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:top]
    return {
        "total": total,
        "status_classes": dict(sorted(by_status.items())),
        "top_paths": by_path.most_common(top),
        "top_talkers": heavy,
        "busiest_hour": hourly.most_common(1)[0] if hourly else None,
    }

    for key, value in report.items():
        print(f"{key}: {value}")

from collections import OrderedDict
from functools import wraps
from threading import RLock


class LRUCache:
    """Thread-safe least-recently-used cache with a fixed capacity."""

    _MISSING = object()

    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        if capacity <= 0:
            raise ValueError("capacity must be positive")
        self.capacity = capacity
        self._data: "OrderedDict[object, object]" = OrderedDict()
        self._lock = RLock()
        self.hits = 0
        self.misses = 0

    def get(self, key, default=None):
        with self._lock:
            try:
                value = self._data[key]
            except KeyError:
                self.misses += 1
                return default
            self._data.move_to_end(key)
            self.hits += 1
            return value

    def put(self, key, value) -> None:
        with self._lock:
            if key in self._data:
                self._data.move_to_end(key)
            self._data[key] = value
            while len(self._data) > self.capacity:
                self._data.popitem(last=False)

    def __len__(self) -> int:
        return len(self._data)

    @property
    def hit_rate(self) -> float:
        total = self.hits + self.misses
        return self.hits / total if total else 0.0


def memoize(capacity: int = 128):
    """Decorator: cache results of a pure function of hashable arguments."""

    def decorate(fn):
        cache = LRUCache(capacity)

        @wraps(fn)
        def wrapper(*args, **kwargs):
            key = (args, tuple(sorted(kwargs.items())))
            value = cache.get(key, LRUCache._MISSING)
            if value is LRUCache._MISSING:
                value = fn(*args, **kwargs)
                cache.put(key, value)
            return value

        wrapper.cache = cache
        return wrapper

    return decorate


@memoize(capacity=64)
def fib(n: int) -> int:
    return n if n < 2 else fib(n - 1) + fib(n - 2)

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Compute per-column summary statistics for a CSV file."""

import argparse
import csv
import statistics
import sys


def numeric_columns(rows, header):
    cols = {name: [] for name in header}
    for row in rows:
        for name, cell in zip(header, row):
            try:
                cols[name].append(float(cell))
            except ValueError:
                cols[name] = None if cols[name] == [] else cols[name]
                break
    return {k: v for k, v in cols.items() if v}


def describe(values):
    values = sorted(values)
    n = len(values)
    q = statistics.quantiles(values, n=4) if n >= 2 else [values[0]] * 3
    return {
        "n": n,
        "min": values[0],
        "q1": q[0],
        "median": q[1],
        "q3": q[2],
        "max": values[-1],
        "mean": statistics.fmean(values),
        "stdev": statistics.stdev(values) if n > 1 else 0.0,
    }


def main():
    ap = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    ap.add_argument("path", nargs="?", default="-")
    ap.add_argument("--delimiter", default=",")
    ap.add_argument("--precision", type=int, default=3)
    args = ap.parse_args()

    stream = sys.stdin if args.path == "-" else open(args.path, newline="")
    with stream:
        reader = csv.reader(stream, delimiter=args.delimiter)
        header = next(reader)
        cols = numeric_columns(reader, header)

    for name, values in cols.items():
        stats = describe(values)
        line = "  ".join(f"{k}={v:.{args.precision}f}" if k != "n" else f"n={v}"
                         for k, v in stats.items())
        print(f"{name:>16}: {line}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

"""A tiny priority task scheduler using a binary heap."""

import heapq
import itertools
import time
from typing import Callable, Optional


class Scheduler:
    def __init__(self, clock: Callable[[], float] = time.monotonic) -> None:
        self._clock = clock
        self._heap = []
        self._counter = itertools.count()
        self._cancelled = set()

    def call_later(self, delay: float, fn: Callable[[], None], priority: int = 0) -> int:
        task_id = next(self._counter)
        when = self._clock() + delay
        heapq.heappush(self._heap, (when, priority, task_id, fn))
        return task_id

    def cancel(self, task_id: int) -> None:
        self._cancelled.add(task_id)

    def next_deadline(self) -> Optional[float]:
        while self._heap and self._heap[0][2] in self._cancelled:
            _, _, tid, _ = heapq.heappop(self._heap)
            self._cancelled.discard(tid)
        return self._heap[0][0] if self._heap else None

    def run_pending(self) -> int:
        """Run every task whose deadline has passed; return how many ran."""
        ran = 0
        now = self._clock()
        while True:
            deadline = self.next_deadline()
            if deadline is None or deadline > now:
                return ran
            _, _, tid, fn = heapq.heappop(self._heap)
            try:
                fn()
            except Exception as exc:  # keep the loop alive
                print(f"task {tid} failed: {exc!r}")
            ran += 1


def _demo():
    fake_now = [0.0]
    sched = Scheduler(clock=lambda: fake_now[0])
    log = []
    sched.call_later(2, lambda: log.append("b"))
    sched.call_later(1, lambda: log.append("a"))
    doomed = sched.call_later(1.5, lambda: log.append("never"))
    sched.cancel(doomed)
    fake_now[0] = 3
    assert sched.run_pending() == 2
    assert log == ["a", "b"], log
    print("scheduler ok")

    _demo()

#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>

void vec_init(vec_t *v) {
    v->data = NULL;
    v->len = 0;
    v->cap = 0;
}

void vec_free(vec_t *v) {
    free(v->data);
    vec_init(v);
}

int vec_push(vec_t *v, int value) {
    if (v->len == v->cap) {
        size_t new_cap = v->cap ? v->cap * 2 : 8;
        int *p = realloc(v->data, new_cap * sizeof *p);
        if (!p) return -1;           /* old buffer is still valid */
        v->data = p;
        v->cap = new_cap;
    }
    v->data[v->len++] = value;
    return 0;
}

int vec_pop(vec_t *v, int *out) {
    if (v->len == 0) return -1;
    *out = v->data[--v->len];
    return 0;
}

static int cmp_int(const void *a, const void *b) {
    int x = *(const int *)a, y = *(const int *)b;
    return (x > y) - (x < y);        /* avoids overflow from x - y */
}

void vec_sort(vec_t *v) {
    qsort(v->data, v->len, sizeof *v->data, cmp_int);
}


/* ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
 * File: hashmap.c -- open addressing, linear probing, string keys
 * ------------------------------------------------------------------------- */
#include <stdint.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>

typedef struct {
    char    *key;       /* NULL = empty, TOMBSTONE = deleted */
    int      value;
} slot_t;

typedef struct {
    slot_t *slots;
    size_t  cap;        /* always a power of two */
    size_t  used;       /* live entries */
    size_t  filled;     /* live entries + tombstones */
} map_t;

static char TOMBSTONE_KEY[] = "<deleted>";
#define TOMBSTONE TOMBSTONE_KEY

/* FNV-1a, 64-bit */
static uint64_t hash_str(const char *s) {
    uint64_t h = 1469598103934665603ULL;
    for (; *s; s++) {
        h ^= (unsigned char)*s;
        h *= 1099511628211ULL;
    }
    return h;
}

static int map_init(map_t *m, size_t cap) {
    m->slots = calloc(cap, sizeof(slot_t));
    if (!m->slots) return -1;
    m->cap = cap;
    m->used = m->filled = 0;
    return 0;
}

static slot_t *map_find(const map_t *m, const char *key, int for_insert) {
    size_t mask = m->cap - 1;
    size_t i = hash_str(key) & mask;
    slot_t *first_tomb = NULL;
    for (;;) {
        slot_t *s = &m->slots[i];
        if (s->key == NULL)
            return (for_insert && first_tomb) ? first_tomb : s;
        if (s->key == TOMBSTONE) {
            if (!first_tomb) first_tomb = s;
        } else if (strcmp(s->key, key) == 0) {
            return s;
        }
        i = (i + 1) & mask;
    }
}

static int map_grow(map_t *m) {
    map_t bigger;
    if (map_init(&bigger, m->cap * 2) != 0) return -1;
    for (size_t i = 0; i < m->cap; i++) {
        slot_t *s = &m->slots[i];
        if (s->key && s->key != TOMBSTONE) {
            slot_t *d = map_find(&bigger, s->key, 1);
            *d = *s;
            bigger.used++;
            bigger.filled++;
        }
    }
    free(m->slots);
    *m = bigger;
    return 0;
}

int map_put(map_t *m, const char *key, int value) {
    /* keep load factor (including tombstones) under 70% */
    if ((m->filled + 1) * 10 > m->cap * 7 && map_grow(m) != 0) return -1;
    slot_t *s = map_find(m, key, 1);
    if (s->key == NULL || s->key == TOMBSTONE) {
        char *copy = strdup(key);
        if (!copy) return -1;
        if (s->key == NULL) m->filled++;
        s->key = copy;
        m->used++;
    }
    s->value = value;
    return 0;
}

int map_get(const map_t *m, const char *key, int *out) {
    slot_t *s = map_find(m, key, 0);
    if (s->key == NULL) return 0;
    *out = s->value;
    return 1;
}

int map_del(map_t *m, const char *key) {
    slot_t *s = map_find(m, key, 0);
    if (s->key == NULL) return 0;
    free(s->key);
    s->key = TOMBSTONE;
    m->used--;
    return 1;
}

int main(void) {
    map_t m;
    if (map_init(&m, 8) != 0) return 1;
    const char *words[] = {"alpha", "beta", "gamma", "delta", "epsilon",
                           "zeta", "eta", "theta", "iota", "kappa"};
    for (int i = 0; i < 10; i++) map_put(&m, words[i], i * i);
    map_del(&m, "gamma");
    int v;
    printf("delta -> %d\n", map_get(&m, "delta", &v) ? v : -1);
    printf("gamma present: %d\n", map_get(&m, "gamma", &v));
    printf("used=%zu cap=%zu\n", m.used, m.cap);
    return 0;
}


/* ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
 * File: calc.c -- recursive descent parser for arithmetic expressions
 *
 * Grammar:
 *   expr   := term   (('+' | '-') term)*
 *   term   := factor (('*' | '/' | '%') factor)*
 *   factor := ('-' | '+') factor | power
 *   power  := atom ('^' factor)?          -- right associative
 *   atom   := NUMBER | IDENT '(' expr ')' | '(' expr ')'
 * ------------------------------------------------------------------------- */
#include <ctype.h>
#include <math.h>
#include <setjmp.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>

typedef struct {
    const char *src;
    const char *p;
    jmp_buf     err;
    char        msg[96];
} parser_t;

static void fail(parser_t *ps, const char *what) {
    snprintf(ps->msg, sizeof ps->msg, "%s at column %d", what, (int)(ps->p - ps->src) + 1);
    longjmp(ps->err, 1);
}

static void skip_ws(parser_t *ps) {
    while (isspace((unsigned char)*ps->p)) ps->p++;
}

static int accept(parser_t *ps, char c) {
    skip_ws(ps);
    if (*ps->p == c) { ps->p++; return 1; }
    return 0;
}

static double parse_expr(parser_t *ps);

static double parse_atom(parser_t *ps) {
    skip_ws(ps);
    if (accept(ps, '(')) {
        double v = parse_expr(ps);
        if (!accept(ps, ')')) fail(ps, "expected ')'");
        return v;
    }
    if (isdigit((unsigned char)*ps->p) || *ps->p == '.') {
        char *end;
        double v = strtod(ps->p, &end);
        ps->p = end;
        return v;
    }
    if (isalpha((unsigned char)*ps->p)) {
        char name[16];
        size_t n = 0;
        while (isalnum((unsigned char)*ps->p) && n + 1 < sizeof name)
            name[n++] = *ps->p++;
        name[n] = '\0';
        if (!accept(ps, '(')) fail(ps, "expected '(' after function name");
        double arg = parse_expr(ps);
        if (!accept(ps, ')')) fail(ps, "expected ')'");
        if (!strcmp(name, "sqrt")) return sqrt(arg);
        if (!strcmp(name, "sin"))  return sin(arg);
        if (!strcmp(name, "cos"))  return cos(arg);
        if (!strcmp(name, "ln"))   return log(arg);
        if (!strcmp(name, "abs"))  return fabs(arg);
        fail(ps, "unknown function");
    }
    fail(ps, "unexpected character");
    return 0.0; /* not reached */
}

static double parse_factor(parser_t *ps);

static double parse_power(parser_t *ps) {
    double base = parse_atom(ps);
    if (accept(ps, '^')) return pow(base, parse_factor(ps));
    return base;
}

static double parse_factor(parser_t *ps) {
    if (accept(ps, '-')) return -parse_factor(ps);
    if (accept(ps, '+')) return parse_factor(ps);
    return parse_power(ps);
}

static double parse_term(parser_t *ps) {
    double v = parse_factor(ps);
    for (;;) {
        if (accept(ps, '*')) v *= parse_factor(ps);
        else if (accept(ps, '/')) {
            double d = parse_factor(ps);
            if (d == 0.0) fail(ps, "division by zero");
            v /= d;
        } else if (accept(ps, '%')) {
            double d = parse_factor(ps);
            if (d == 0.0) fail(ps, "modulo by zero");
            v = fmod(v, d);
        } else return v;
    }
}

static double parse_expr(parser_t *ps) {
    double v = parse_term(ps);
    for (;;) {
        if (accept(ps, '+')) v += parse_term(ps);
        else if (accept(ps, '-')) v -= parse_term(ps);
        else return v;
    }
}

int calc_eval(const char *text, double *result, char *errbuf, size_t errlen) {
    parser_t ps = { .src = text, .p = text };
    if (setjmp(ps.err)) {
        snprintf(errbuf, errlen, "%s", ps.msg);
        return -1;
    }
    double v = parse_expr(&ps);
    skip_ws(&ps);
    if (*ps.p != '\0') fail(&ps, "trailing input");
    *result = v;
    return 0;
}

int main(int argc, char **argv) {

    for (int i = 1; i < argc; i++) {
        double r;
        if (calc_eval(argv[i], &r, err, sizeof err) == 0)
            printf("%s = %.10g\n", argv[i], r);
        else
            fprintf(stderr, "%s: error: %s\n", argv[i], err);
    }
    return 0;
}

// File: ring_buffer.hpp -- single-producer single-consumer lock-free queue
// ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
#pragma once

#include <array>
#include <atomic>
#include <cstddef>
#include <optional>
#include <type_traits>

template <typename T, std::size_t N>
class SpscRing {
    static_assert((N & (N - 1)) == 0, "capacity must be a power of two");
    static_assert(std::is_nothrow_move_constructible_v<T>);

public:
    bool push(T value) noexcept {
        const auto head = head_.load(std::memory_order_relaxed);
        const auto next = (head + 1) & kMask;
        if (next == tail_.load(std::memory_order_acquire)) {
            return false;  // full
        }
        buf_[head] = std::move(value);
        head_.store(next, std::memory_order_release);
        return true;
    }

    std::optional<T> pop() noexcept {
        const auto tail = tail_.load(std::memory_order_relaxed);
        if (tail == head_.load(std::memory_order_acquire)) {
            return std::nullopt;  // empty
        }
        T out = std::move(buf_[tail]);
        tail_.store((tail + 1) & kMask, std::memory_order_release);
        return out;
    }

    std::size_t size() const noexcept {
        const auto h = head_.load(std::memory_order_acquire);
        const auto t = tail_.load(std::memory_order_acquire);
        return (h - t) & kMask;
    }

private:
    static constexpr std::size_t kMask = N - 1;

    // Keep the two indices on separate cache lines to avoid false sharing.
    alignas(64) std::atomic<std::size_t> head_{0};
    alignas(64) std::atomic<std::size_t> tail_{0};
    std::array<T, N> buf_{};
};

// File: bst.cpp -- an unbalanced binary search tree with iterative traversal
// ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
#include <functional>
#include <iostream>
#include <memory>
#include <stack>
#include <string>

template <typename K, typename V>
class Bst {
    struct Node {
        K key;
        V value;
        std::unique_ptr<Node> left, right;
        Node(K k, V v) : key(std::move(k)), value(std::move(v)) {}
    };

public:
    bool insert(K key, V value) {
        std::unique_ptr<Node>* cur = &root_;
        while (*cur) {
            if (key < (*cur)->key)       cur = &(*cur)->left;
            else if ((*cur)->key < key)  cur = &(*cur)->right;
            else { (*cur)->value = std::move(value); return false; }
        }
        *cur = std::make_unique<Node>(std::move(key), std::move(value));
        ++size_;
        return true;
    }

    const V* find(const K& key) const {
        const Node* n = root_.get();
        while (n) {
            if (key < n->key)      n = n->left.get();
            else if (n->key < key) n = n->right.get();
            else return &n->value;
        }
        return nullptr;
    }

    // In-order traversal without recursion, so deep trees cannot blow the stack.
    void for_each(const std::function<void(const K&, const V&)>& fn) const {
        std::stack<const Node*> pending;
        const Node* n = root_.get();
        while (n || !pending.empty()) {
            while (n) { pending.push(n); n = n->left.get(); }
            n = pending.top();
            pending.pop();
            fn(n->key, n->value);
            n = n->right.get();
        }
    }

    std::size_t size() const { return size_; }

    int height() const { return height(root_.get()); }

private:
    static int height(const Node* n) {
        if (!n) return 0;
        return 1 + std::max(height(n->left.get()), height(n->right.get()));
    }

    std::unique_ptr<Node> root_;
    std::size_t size_ = 0;
};

int main() {
    Bst<std::string, int> tree;
    for (const char* w : {"pear", "apple", "quince", "banana", "cherry", "fig"})
        tree.insert(w, static_cast<int>(std::string(w).size()));
    tree.for_each([](const std::string& k, int v) {
        std::cout << k << " (" << v << ")\n";
    });
    std::cout << "height=" << tree.height() << " size=" << tree.size() << '\n';
    if (const int* p = tree.find("cherry")) std::cout << "cherry -> " << *p << '\n';
}

// y = a*x + y, grid-stride loop so any grid size handles any n.
__global__ void saxpy(int n, float a, const float* __restrict__ x,
                      float* __restrict__ y) {
    for (int i = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x; i < n;
         i += gridDim.x * blockDim.x) {
        y[i] = fmaf(a, x[i], y[i]);
    }

// Sum reduction: each warp reduces with shuffles, then warp leaders combine
// through shared memory. Launch with blockDim.x a multiple of 32, <= 1024.
__global__ void block_sum(const float* __restrict__ in, float* __restrict__ out,
                          int n) {
    __shared__ float partial[32];
    float v = 0.0f;
    for (int i = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x; i < n;
         i += gridDim.x * blockDim.x) {
        v += in[i];
    }
#pragma unroll
    for (int off = 16; off > 0; off >>= 1) {
        v += __shfl_down_sync(0xffffffffu, v, off);
    }
    const int lane = threadIdx.x & 31;
    const int warp = threadIdx.x >> 5;
    if (lane == 0) partial[warp] = v;
    __syncthreads();

    if (warp == 0) {
        const int nwarps = blockDim.x >> 5;
        v = (lane < nwarps) ? partial[lane] : 0.0f;
#pragma unroll
        for (int off = 16; off > 0; off >>= 1) {
            v += __shfl_down_sync(0xffffffffu, v, off);
        }
        if (lane == 0) atomicAdd(out, v);
    }
}

// Tiled matrix transpose. The +1 padding on the shared tile avoids bank
// conflicts when threads in a warp read down a column.
constexpr int TILE = 32;

__global__ void transpose(const float* __restrict__ in, float* __restrict__ out,
                          int rows, int cols) {
    __shared__ float tile[TILE][TILE + 1];

    int x = blockIdx.x * TILE + threadIdx.x;
    int y = blockIdx.y * TILE + threadIdx.y;
    if (x < cols && y < rows) tile[threadIdx.y][threadIdx.x] = in[y * cols + x];
    __syncthreads();

    x = blockIdx.y * TILE + threadIdx.x;   // swapped block indices
    y = blockIdx.x * TILE + threadIdx.y;
    if (x < rows && y < cols) out[y * rows + x] = tile[threadIdx.x][threadIdx.y];
}

int main() {
    const int n = 1 << 22;
    float *x, *y, *sum;
    CUDA_CHECK(cudaMallocManaged(&x, n * sizeof(float)));
    CUDA_CHECK(cudaMallocManaged(&y, n * sizeof(float)));
    CUDA_CHECK(cudaMallocManaged(&sum, sizeof(float)));
    for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) { x[i] = 1.0f; y[i] = 2.0f; }
    *sum = 0.0f;

    saxpy<<<256, 256>>>(n, 3.0f, x, y);          // y becomes 5.0 everywhere
    block_sum<<<128, 256>>>(y, sum, n);
    CUDA_CHECK(cudaDeviceSynchronize());

    std::printf("sum = %.1f (expected %.1f)\n", *sum, 5.0f * n);
    cudaFree(x); cudaFree(y); cudaFree(sum);
    return 0;
}

# File: store/kv.py -- a small SQLite-backed key/value store with TTLs
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
"""Persistent key/value storage with optional expiry.

Values are JSON-encoded, so anything ``json.dumps`` accepts can be stored.
A single connection is shared and guarded by a lock, which is plenty for the
modest write rates this module is meant for.
"""

import json
import sqlite3
import threading
import time
from contextlib import contextmanager
from typing import Any, Callable, Iterator, Optional

_SCHEMA = """
CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS kv (
    key        TEXT PRIMARY KEY,
    value      TEXT NOT NULL,
    expires_at REAL
);
CREATE INDEX IF NOT EXISTS kv_expires ON kv(expires_at)
    WHERE expires_at IS NOT NULL;
"""


class KVStore:
    def __init__(self, path: str = ":memory:",
                 now: Callable[[], float] = time.time) -> None:
        self._now = now
        self._lock = threading.Lock()
        self._db = sqlite3.connect(path, check_same_thread=False)
        self._db.executescript(_SCHEMA)

    @contextmanager
    def _tx(self) -> Iterator[sqlite3.Cursor]:
        with self._lock:
            cur = self._db.cursor()
            try:
                yield cur
                self._db.commit()
            except BaseException:
                self._db.rollback()
                raise
            finally:
                cur.close()

    def set(self, key: str, value: Any, ttl: Optional[float] = None) -> None:
        expires = self._now() + ttl if ttl is not None else None
        with self._tx() as cur:
            cur.execute(
                "INSERT INTO kv(key, value, expires_at) VALUES (?, ?, ?) "
                "ON CONFLICT(key) DO UPDATE SET value=excluded.value, "
                "expires_at=excluded.expires_at",
                (key, json.dumps(value), expires),
            )

    def get(self, key: str, default: Any = None) -> Any:
        with self._tx() as cur:
            row = cur.execute(
                "SELECT value, expires_at FROM kv WHERE key = ?", (key,)
            ).fetchone()
            if row is None:
                return default
            value, expires = row
            if expires is not None and expires <= self._now():
                cur.execute("DELETE FROM kv WHERE key = ?", (key,))
                return default
            return json.loads(value)

    def delete(self, key: str) -> bool:
        with self._tx() as cur:
            cur.execute("DELETE FROM kv WHERE key = ?", (key,))
            return cur.rowcount > 0

    def incr(self, key: str, amount: int = 1) -> int:
        """Atomically add ``amount`` to a numeric value (missing counts as 0)."""
        with self._tx() as cur:
            row = cur.execute("SELECT value FROM kv WHERE key = ?", (key,)).fetchone()
            current = json.loads(row[0]) if row else 0
            if not isinstance(current, (int, float)):
                raise TypeError(f"value at {key!r} is not numeric")
            new = current + amount
            cur.execute(
                "INSERT INTO kv(key, value) VALUES (?, ?) "
                "ON CONFLICT(key) DO UPDATE SET value=excluded.value",
                (key, json.dumps(new)),
            )
            return new

    def purge_expired(self) -> int:
        with self._tx() as cur:
            cur.execute(
                "DELETE FROM kv WHERE expires_at IS NOT NULL AND expires_at <= ?",
                (self._now(),),
            )
            return cur.rowcount

    def keys(self, prefix: str = "") -> list:
        like = prefix.replace("\\", "\\\\").replace("%", "\\%").replace("_", "\\_") + "%"
        with self._tx() as cur:
            rows = cur.execute(
                "SELECT key FROM kv WHERE key LIKE ? ESCAPE '\\' "
                "AND (expires_at IS NULL OR expires_at > ?) ORDER BY key",
                (like, self._now()),
            ).fetchall()
        return [r[0] for r in rows]

    def close(self) -> None:
        with self._lock:
            self._db.close()

import functools
import logging
import random
import time

log = logging.getLogger(__name__)


def retry(attempts=5, base_delay=0.2, max_delay=10.0,
          exceptions=(Exception,), sleep=time.sleep, rng=random.random):
    """Retry a callable, doubling the delay each time and adding jitter.

    The delay before attempt k (k starting at 1 for the first retry) is drawn
    uniformly from [0, min(max_delay, base_delay * 2**(k-1))]. This is the
    "full jitter" strategy, which spreads out clients that failed together.
    """
    if attempts < 1:
        raise ValueError("attempts must be >= 1")

    def decorator(fn):
        @functools.wraps(fn)
        def wrapper(*args, **kwargs):
            last = None
            for attempt in range(1, attempts + 1):
                try:
                    return fn(*args, **kwargs)
                except exceptions as exc:
                    last = exc
                    if attempt == attempts:
                        break
                    ceiling = min(max_delay, base_delay * 2 ** (attempt - 1))
                    delay = rng() * ceiling
                    log.warning("%s failed (%s); retry %d/%d in %.2fs",
                                fn.__name__, exc, attempt, attempts - 1, delay)
                    sleep(delay)
            raise last

        return wrapper

    return decorator


if __name__ == "__main__":

        if calls["n"] < 3:
            raise ConnectionError("temporary failure")
        return "ok after %d calls" % calls["n"]

THE HANSEATIC LEAGUE AND THE ECONOMICS OF MEDIEVAL BALTIC TRADE

The Hanseatic League was a commercial and defensive confederation of merchant guilds and market towns that dominated trade across northern Europe from roughly the thirteenth to the seventeenth century. It was never a state in any modern sense. It had no capital, no standing army, no permanent treasury and no single ruler. Its closest institution to a government was the Hansetag, an assembly of delegates that met irregularly in Lübeck and often failed to reach agreement. Yet for more than three hundred years this loose association of perhaps two hundred towns, ranging from Novgorod in the east to London and Bruges in the west, controlled the movement of grain, timber, furs, salt, herring, cloth and beer across the North Sea and the Baltic.

The origins of the league lie in the practical needs of merchants traveling in dangerous and legally uncertain territory. In the twelfth century, German traders from towns such as Cologne and Lübeck began forming associations to protect one another on the road and at sea. Lübeck, founded in 1143 and refounded in 1159 by Henry the Lion of Saxony, soon became the pivotal port. Its location at the base of the Jutland peninsula allowed goods to be carried overland from the Baltic to Hamburg on the North Sea in a few days, avoiding the long and storm-ridden voyage around Denmark. A treaty between Lübeck and Hamburg in 1241 is often cited as an early landmark, though historians debate whether it should be called the founding of the league.

The commodities the Hanse carried explain much of its wealth. From the east came rye and wheat from the Prussian and Polish plains, timber, tar and pitch for shipbuilding, wax, amber, and furs from Russia. From the west came woolen cloth from Flanders and England, wine from the Rhineland and France, and manufactured metalwork. Between these flows moved salt and fish. The herring fisheries off Scania, in what is now southern Sweden, produced enormous catches each autumn, and the fish had to be salted within hours to keep. Salt came from Lüneburg and later from Bay of Biscay sources, carried north in the same ships that brought back barrels of preserved herring for the Catholic calendar's many fast days. Few trades combined predictable demand with so little technological risk, and the profits financed the great brick churches and merchants' halls that still define the skylines of Lübeck, Stralsund and Gdańsk.

The ship that made this trade practical was the cog, a single-masted, flat-bottomed vessel with high sides and a stern-mounted rudder. A typical cog of the fourteenth century carried perhaps 100 to 200 tons, far less than later carracks, but it was cheap to build, easy to handle with a small crew, and capacious enough to make Baltic voyages profitable. A well-preserved example, the Bremen Cog, was found in the Weser River in 1962 and dated by dendrochronology to 1380. It is now displayed in the German Maritime Museum, and its construction reveals that medieval shipwrights relied on a remarkably standardized design, assembling hulls from oak planks fastened by iron nails and caulked with moss soaked in pine tar.

The league's political strength rested on its ability to apply collective economic pressure. When a ruler mistreated Hanseatic merchants, the member towns could impose a trade embargo, a measure known as the Verhansung or, in the case of a disobedient member, expulsion from the league. In 1280 and again in 1358 the towns suspended trade with Flanders to secure better privileges at Bruges. In the Danish war of 1367 to 1370, a coalition known as the Cologne Confederation defeated King Valdemar IV Atterdag and forced the Treaty of Stralsund in 1370, which granted the league control over key Scanian fortresses and a say in Danish royal succession. This was the high point of its power. For a few decades, a federation of merchants had dictated terms to a European king.

The league maintained overseas trading posts known as Kontore. The four principal ones were the Peterhof in Novgorod, the Steelyard in London, the Bryggen in Bergen and the Hanseatic Kontor in Bruges. Each operated almost like a small self-governing town within the host city. Merchants lived under strict rules: in Bergen, young apprentices were required to remain unmarried and were subject to a regime of hazing rituals so notorious that the Norwegian historian Jens Christian Hansen described the place as a school of endurance. The German presence in Bergen was so dominant that for centuries stockfish exported from northern Norway was sold almost entirely through Hanseatic hands, and the wooden warehouses along the harbor, rebuilt after fires in 1702 and earlier, are today a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Why did such a successful system decline? Historians usually point to several overlapping causes. First, the rise of territorial states in the sixteenth century, such as Sweden under Gustav Vasa, Denmark, Russia and England, reduced the room for merchant confederations to bargain on their own terms. Second, the Dutch, who had been junior partners in the Baltic trade, developed larger and cheaper ships, notably the fluyt, and undercut Hanseatic prices. By 1600 a majority of ships passing through the Danish Sound tolls at Elsinore were Dutch. Third, the shift of the world's economic center toward the Atlantic after Columbus and da Gama left the Baltic relatively less central. And fourth, the league's decentralized structure made it nearly impossible to coordinate a response. The last formal Hansetag was held in 1669, attended by just nine cities. Lübeck, Hamburg and Bremen continued the tradition into the modern era, and the three cities still carry the title "Free and Hanseatic City" in their official names.

The Hanseatic experience is often discussed as an early example of how trade networks can generate shared prosperity without a central authority. It also illustrates a cautionary point: networks held together mainly by mutual benefit can unravel when external conditions shift the balance of advantage. In the 1990s, the name was revived in the New Hanseatic League, an informal grouping of northern European finance ministers who coordinated positions on European fiscal policy, a modest echo of the original merchant assembly in Lübeck.

HOW A BIRD'S FLIGHT FEATHER WORKS

A feather is one of the most intricate structures produced by any vertebrate. It is made of beta-keratin, a protein also found in reptile scales and claws, but its architecture is unlike any other biological material. Understanding the flight feather of a bird requires attention to three nested levels: the central shaft, the barbs that branch from it, and the microscopic barbules that branch again from each barb.

The shaft consists of two parts. The hollow base, called the calamus, is embedded in the skin of the wing. The solid, tapering continuation above it is the rachis. Along both sides of the rachis, parallel barbs extend at an angle, forming the flat blade called the vane. In a typical pigeon primary feather, there may be several hundred barbs on each side. If you look at one barb under a microscope, you will find that it carries two rows of even smaller branches: the distal barbules, which bear tiny hooks called barbicels, and the proximal barbules, which have a groove or ridge that catches those hooks. It is this hook-and-groove arrangement, rather like a natural zipper, that holds adjacent barbs together and creates a continuous, air-resistant surface.

When a bird preens, it draws the feather through its beak, re-zipping any barbs that have come apart. This is why a ruffled feather can be restored in a few seconds. A single large flight feather may contain over a million barbules, and the system is robust but not indestructible. When a feather is damaged beyond preening's capacity, the bird must wait for the next molt, during which old feathers are shed and new ones grow in. Large birds such as swans lose all their flight feathers at once and are temporarily flightless, so they molt in safe, remote lakes. Smaller species often molt sequentially so that they retain the ability to fly.

The feather is also an aerodynamic device with an asymmetric profile. In the primaries, the outer vane (the leading edge) is much narrower than the inner vane. This asymmetry allows each feather to act like a small airfoil, twisting under load so that air flows smoothly across the surface. Slotted wingtips, in which the outer primaries spread apart like the fingers of a hand, reduce induced drag, the energy lost to the vortices that form at the wingtip. Eagles, vultures and storks all use this configuration while soaring. Modern aircraft designers copied the idea when they added winglets to airliner wings in the 1970s and 1980s, reducing fuel consumption by a few percent.

Some of the most striking colors in nature come not from pigment but from feather structure. The brilliant blue of a kingfisher or a bluebird contains no blue pigment at all. Instead, the barbs contain a spongy layer of keratin and air bubbles with dimensions close to the wavelength of blue light, so that other wavelengths cancel out and blue is scattered back to the eye. This is called structural coloration, and the same physics gives the iridescent greens of hummingbirds and the shimmering necks of pigeons. Reds and yellows, by contrast, usually come from carotenoid pigments that the bird obtains from its diet. Flamingos are pink because their food, algae and small crustaceans, is rich in carotenoids, and captive flamingos fed a plain diet fade to a dull white unless supplemented.

The evolutionary origin of feathers has been a major subject of paleontology since the discovery of Archaeopteryx in 1861 in the limestone quarries of Solnhofen, Bavaria. That specimen, about 150 million years old, showed unmistakable feathers on a skeleton that was otherwise reptilian. Later discoveries in Liaoning Province, China, beginning in the 1990s, revealed many feathered non-avian dinosaurs, such as Sinosauropteryx and Microraptor, some with simple filaments and others with complex vanes. The current consensus is that feathers evolved first for insulation or display and were only later co-opted for flight. Pigment-bearing organelles called melanosomes preserved in fossil feathers have even allowed researchers to reconstruct the likely colors of some extinct species. Anchiornis, a small dinosaur about 160 million years old, appears to have had gray-black body plumage with a reddish crest and white wing feathers with black tips.

THE ECONOMICS OF A SIMPLE TRADE: COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE

Imagine two countries, each of which can produce two goods, wine and cloth. In the first country, which we will call Alpha, one worker can produce either 10 bottles of wine or 20 meters of cloth in a week. In the second country, Beta, a worker can produce either 4 bottles of wine or 4 meters of cloth in the same time. Alpha is better at both tasks. Does trade between them still make sense?

The answer, established by David Ricardo in his 1817 work On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, is yes, and the reasoning is one of the most counterintuitive and important ideas in economics. The key is that what matters for gains from trade is not absolute productivity but opportunity cost. In Alpha, producing one bottle of wine requires giving up 2 meters of cloth (20 divided by 10). In Beta, producing one bottle of wine requires giving up 1 meter of cloth (4 divided by 4). So Beta has the lower opportunity cost of wine, and Alpha has the lower opportunity cost of cloth. Beta has a comparative advantage in wine, and Alpha in cloth, even though Alpha is more productive in both absolute terms.

Suppose each country has 100 workers and, in the absence of trade, splits them evenly. Alpha produces 500 bottles of wine and 1,000 meters of cloth. Beta produces 200 bottles of wine and 200 meters of cloth. Total world output is 700 bottles and 1,200 meters. Now suppose the countries specialize completely: Alpha puts all 100 workers into cloth, producing 2,000 meters, and Beta puts all 100 workers into wine, producing 400 bottles. Total output is now 400 bottles and 2,000 meters. If they trade at a rate of one bottle for 1.5 meters, both can end up with more of both goods than before. Beta, for instance, could trade 200 bottles to Alpha for 300 meters of cloth, leaving it with 200 bottles and 300 meters, versus the 200 bottles and 200 meters it had at the start. Alpha would have 1,700 meters of cloth and 200 bottles, rather than 1,000 meters and 500 bottles, which is not obviously better, so the exact terms matter, and in a fuller treatment one would calculate the range of exchange rates that make both parties strictly better off.

The principle has limits. It assumes full employment, constant returns to scale, no transport costs and mobile labor within countries but not between them. Real economies violate each of these. Workers displaced by imports may not find new jobs easily, as the "China shock" literature of David Autor, David Dorn and Gordon Hanson documented: between 1999 and 2011, import competition from China was associated with the loss of roughly 2 to 2.4 million American jobs, concentrated in specific communities that did not rebound quickly. Gains from trade are real in aggregate, but they are not distributed evenly, and a policy that ignores the losers will eventually lose political support.

Even so, comparative advantage explains much of observed trade patterns. Countries with abundant skilled labor tend to export complex manufactures; countries with abundant arable land export agricultural goods; and small economies with specific natural resources specialize intensively, as when Chile exports copper, which accounts for roughly half of its goods exports, or when Bangladesh exports ready-made garments, which account for over 80 percent. Economists since Ricardo have refined the theory, notably Eli Heckscher and Bertil Ohlin in the 1920s, who linked trade patterns to factor endowments, and Paul Krugman in the late 1970s, who showed how economies of scale and consumer love of variety can produce trade between countries that look similar. Krugman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in 2008 for this work.

THE GEOGRAPHY OF THE ATACAMA DESERT

The Atacama Desert stretches along the Pacific coast of South America for roughly 1,000 kilometers, mostly within northern Chile, and is widely described as the driest non-polar place on Earth. Some weather stations in its core, near Arica and María Elena, have recorded no measurable rain for periods of several years, and the average annual rainfall at the driest sites is less than 1 millimeter. A few locations have gone, according to anecdotal records, more than four hundred years without significant precipitation, although the exact claims should be treated carefully because the instrumental record covers only a little over a century.

Several factors combine to produce this extreme aridity. The first is the rain shadow of the Andes to the east, which blocks moisture from the Amazon basin. The second is the Humboldt Current, a cold ocean current that flows north along the Chilean coast and cools the air above it. Cold air holds little moisture and the resulting temperature inversion suppresses the vertical air movement needed to form rain clouds. The third is the persistent South Pacific anticyclone, a high-pressure system that keeps the atmosphere stable. These conditions have persisted, scientists believe, for at least 10 to 15 million years, making the Atacama the oldest continuously arid landscape on the planet.

Though lifeless in appearance, the desert is not empty. Along the coast, a thick fog called the camanchaca rolls in from the ocean most mornings, and certain plants and lichens depend on it for water. Fog-catching nets, large mesh screens that collect droplets and channel them into tanks, have been used in villages such as Chungungo since the 1990s to provide drinking water. Farther inland, flamingos nest on high-altitude salt flats such as the Salar de Atacama, where brine pools are fed by melting snow from the Andes. The salar covers about 3,000 square kilometers and contains one of the largest known lithium reserves in the world, extracted by pumping brine into shallow evaporation ponds where sunlight concentrates it over many months.

The Atacama's dryness has made it extraordinarily valuable for astronomy. At an altitude of about 5,000 meters on the Chajnantor plateau, the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, known as ALMA, operates 66 high-precision antennas that together simulate a single telescope up to 16 kilometers across. The very low atmospheric water vapor is crucial because water absorbs the submillimeter wavelengths the array observes. Nearby, the European Southern Observatory operates the Very Large Telescope at Cerro Paranal, and is building the Extremely Large Telescope on Cerro Armazones, which will have a primary mirror 39 meters in diameter composed of 798 hexagonal segments. Because the skies are so clear and so dark, an estimated 40 percent of the world's astronomical collecting area is expected to be in northern Chile by the end of this decade.

Humans have lived in the region for thousands of years. The Chinchorro people, who inhabited the coast from about 7000 BCE, produced the oldest known artificially mummified bodies, preceding the better-known Egyptian practice by about two thousand years. In the nineteenth century the desert became the center of a boom in sodium nitrate, or saltpeter, used for fertilizer and explosives. The War of the Pacific from 1879 to 1884 between Chile and an alliance of Peru and Bolivia was fought largely over control of these deposits, and Chile's victory transferred the region, and Bolivia's access to the sea, to Chilean control. The abandoned nitrate towns of Humberstone and Santa Laura, with their rusting machinery and empty theatres, are now museums.

Mars analog research is another reason scientists visit. Soils in the Atacama's core contain so little organic material that standard methods struggle to detect life, and NASA has tested instruments here for use on Mars missions. In 2003, researchers led by Rafael Navarro-González reported that some Atacama soils contained such low levels of bacteria that they resembled Martian samples tested by the Viking landers in 1976. The comparison helped explain why Viking's biological experiments had produced ambiguous results.

WHY THE SKY IS BLUE, AND WHY SUNSETS ARE RED

The color of the daytime sky is a consequence of how light interacts with the molecules of the atmosphere. Sunlight appears white but is in fact a mixture of all visible wavelengths, ranging from violet at about 380 nanometers to red at about 700 nanometers. When this light enters the atmosphere, it encounters nitrogen and oxygen molecules, each far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. A small particle like this scatters light in a way described by Lord Rayleigh in the 1870s: the intensity of scattered light is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength.

The practical consequence of that fourth-power law is dramatic. Blue light at 450 nanometers is scattered roughly (700/450)^4, or about 5.9 times, more strongly than red light at 700 nanometers. Violet light, at 400 nanometers, is scattered even more. So as sunlight passes through the atmosphere, the shorter wavelengths are bounced in all directions, and when you look at any part of the sky away from the sun, you see this scattered light arriving from all around you.

A natural question is why the sky is not violet, since violet is scattered more than blue. There are three reasons. First, the sun emits less violet light than blue, so there is less to scatter. Second, the upper atmosphere absorbs some of the violet and ultraviolet. Third, and most important, the human eye is far less sensitive to violet than to blue. Our three types of cone cells respond most strongly to roughly 420, 534 and 564 nanometers, and the combined signal from the scattered spectrum is perceived as a pale, saturated blue rather than violet.

At sunrise and sunset, the sun sits close to the horizon, and its light must pass through much more air to reach your eye, perhaps 40 times more than at noon. Along that long path, most of the blue and green light has been scattered out of the beam and away in other directions. What remains is dominated by the longer wavelengths, orange and red. This is why a sunset looks red, and why the sky nearby takes on that color. Dust, smoke and volcanic aerosols can enhance the effect. After the eruption of Krakatoa in 1883, observers around the world reported unusually vivid red sunsets for months, and some historians have linked the glowing sky in Edvard Munch's painting The Scream to this phenomenon, though that attribution is debated.

Clouds are white for a different reason. Water droplets in clouds are much larger than the wavelength of light, typically 10 to 20 micrometers across, and they scatter all wavelengths nearly equally in a process called Mie scattering. Equal scattering of all colors gives white. Thick storm clouds, on the other hand, can look dark gray because so much light is absorbed or scattered upward before it reaches the ground.

The same physics explains why distant mountains appear bluish and why a glass of water with a drop of milk looks blue when you shine a light from the side and yellowish when you look through it. Both are demonstrations of selective scattering of short wavelengths by small particles.

HOW A VACCINE TRAINS THE IMMUNE SYSTEM

The human immune system has two broad arms. The innate arm responds within minutes to hours to any generic sign of invasion, using cells such as neutrophils and macrophages that recognize broad molecular patterns common to many microbes. The adaptive arm is slower, taking days to mount a first response, but it is far more precise and, critically, it has memory. Vaccines work by exploiting that memory.

When a pathogen enters the body for the first time, specialized cells called dendritic cells engulf some of it and display fragments, known as antigens, on their surface. These cells travel to lymph nodes, where they present the antigens to T lymphocytes. A tiny fraction of the T cells, perhaps one in a hundred thousand, carry receptors that happen to fit that particular antigen. Those cells are selected, activated and begin to multiply, producing millions of identical copies in a process called clonal expansion. Meanwhile, B lymphocytes with fitting receptors also multiply and differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies, proteins that bind the pathogen and mark it for destruction or neutralize it directly.

After the infection is cleared, most of these expanded cells die off, but a small number remain as memory cells that can persist for years or decades. If the same pathogen appears again, the memory cells respond within a day or two, much faster and more strongly than during the first exposure. The person usually experiences no symptoms at all. This is the principle that Edward Jenner stumbled on in 1796 when he inoculated a boy with material from a cowpox lesion and observed that the boy later resisted smallpox.

Modern vaccines achieve the same result without exposing the person to a dangerous infection. Some use weakened, or attenuated, live viruses, as in the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine. Others use killed whole organisms, purified proteins, or polysaccharide fragments, often linked to a carrier protein to provoke a stronger T cell response. Since 2020, messenger RNA vaccines have become widely known. These deliver instructions for making a single viral protein, such as the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles that fuse with cells. The cells then temporarily produce the protein, display it on their surface, and train the immune system. The mRNA itself is degraded within days. Karikó and Weissman, who developed the modified nucleosides that let the RNA avoid triggering excessive inflammation, received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2023.

Many vaccines require more than one dose, because the first dose, called the priming dose, generates a modest response, and subsequent boosters deepen it by stimulating affinity maturation. In that process, B cells in structures called germinal centers repeatedly mutate their antibody genes, and the cells whose antibodies bind most tightly to the antigen are preferentially selected. Over weeks to months, the average binding strength can increase a hundredfold or more.

Herd immunity arises when enough people in a population are immune that an outbreak cannot sustain itself. The threshold depends on the basic reproduction number, R0, the average number of people infected by one case in a fully susceptible population. The simple formula for the threshold is 1 minus 1/R0. For measles, with R0 of 12 to 18, about 92 to 95 percent of the population needs to be immune. For a disease with R0 of 2, the threshold is only 50 percent. This is why even small declines in measles vaccination can lead to outbreaks, as in the United States in 2019 and again in 2025, when case counts reached levels not seen in decades.

THE PRINTING PRESS AND THE SPEED OF IDEAS

Before the 1450s, a book in Europe was an object made by hand. A trained scribe working in a monastery or a commercial workshop might copy a Bible in about a year, and the result would cost roughly as much as a modest house. The largest library in Europe in the fourteenth century, the papal collection at Avignon, held some two thousand volumes, and the University of Cambridge in 1424 owned fewer than two hundred. Reading was limited by cost and scarcity as much as by literacy.

Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith from Mainz, combined several existing technologies into a working system sometime around 1450. The screw press had long been used for making wine and olive oil. Oil-based ink, thicker than the water-based inks used by scribes, adhered well to metal. Paper, imported from China through the Islamic world and manufactured in Europe since the twelfth century, was far cheaper than parchment. Gutenberg's decisive contribution was movable type cast from an alloy of lead, tin and antimony. Each letter was made by pouring molten metal into a reusable brass mold, called a matrix, so that thousands of identical pieces of type could be produced quickly and arranged, then rearranged, to print any text. The metal was hard enough to withstand thousands of impressions but soft enough to melt down and recast.

The first major product was the 42-line Bible, completed around 1455. About 180 copies are thought to have been printed, some on vellum and most on paper, and 49 survive in whole or in part. Gutenberg himself did not profit. A lawsuit brought by his financier, Johann Fust, led to the seizure of his equipment in 1455, and the business passed to Fust and his son-in-law Peter Schöffer. Yet the technology spread with extraordinary speed. By 1470 there were presses in Italy, France and Switzerland. By 1480 there were presses in more than a hundred European towns. By 1500, historians estimate that between 15 and 20 million volumes had been printed in about 250 towns, which is more than all the books produced by hand in Europe during the previous thousand years.

Price is the clearest measure of the change. A bound book that cost the equivalent of several months of a craftsman's wages in 1400 might cost a few days' pay a century later. Pamphlets and broadsides, printed on a single sheet, cost little more than a loaf of bread. This sharp drop in price transformed who could read and what they read. Printers discovered that the market for Latin theology was limited and that vernacular works, almanacs, devotional guides and cheap romances sold more reliably. In Venice, Aldus Manutius introduced the small octavo format in 1501, producing portable editions of classical authors and inventing italic type to fit more words on a page.

The printing press also played a part in the Reformation. When Martin Luther posted his Ninety-five Theses in 1517, which according to tradition he nailed to the door of the castle church at Wittenberg, the document was quickly reprinted in several cities without his involvement. Within two weeks, copies had spread across Germany, and within two months, across Europe. Between 1518 and 1520 Luther's German writings were printed in perhaps 300,000 copies, an astonishing figure for the era. His opponents recognized the danger; in 1559 the Catholic Church issued its first Index of Forbidden Books. But censorship proved difficult to enforce when a banned text could be reprinted in a different jurisdiction within weeks.

Historians such as Elizabeth Eisenstein, in her 1979 study The Printing Press as an Agent of Change, have argued that printing created a new kind of fixity: for the first time, scholars in different cities could consult identical editions and cite pages. Errors could be identified and corrected collectively, and scientific tables, maps and anatomical diagrams could be reproduced with precision. Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica in 1543 and Copernicus's De revolutionibus the same year both depended on the press. Other scholars, including Adrian Johns, have cautioned that early printed books were often unreliable, pirated, or sloppily produced, and that fixity was an achievement of later institutions, not an automatic effect of the technology. The truth likely lies between the two.

What is not in doubt is the pace. Gutenberg's workshop produced, at best, a few hundred pages a day. A mid-nineteenth century steam-driven rotary press could print 20,000 sheets an hour. The telegraph, the penny newspaper and the mass-market novel followed. Each step in the acceleration of information has stirred anxiety about overload, distraction and the loss of older forms of memory, anxieties that sound strikingly familiar to anyone who has read the complaints of the sixteenth-century scholar Conrad Gessner, who in 1545 lamented the "confusing and harmful abundance of books."

ENTROPY AND THE ARROW OF TIME

The fundamental laws of physics, with a few subtle exceptions, are symmetric in time. Newton's equations, Maxwell's equations and the Schrödinger equation work equally well whether time runs forward or backward. If you filmed two billiard balls colliding and played the film in reverse, the reversed motion would also obey the laws of mechanics. Yet our everyday world is not symmetric. Eggs break but do not spontaneously reassemble; ice melts in warm water but does not form from it; we remember the past but not the future. Something must break the symmetry, and the usual answer is entropy.

The concept of entropy emerged in the nineteenth century from the study of heat engines. Rudolf Clausius coined the word in 1865, defining a change in entropy as the heat transferred to a system divided by its absolute temperature, in the reversible case. His statement of the second law of thermodynamics was that the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, never the other way, unless work is done.

Ludwig Boltzmann gave this macroscopic idea a microscopic meaning in the 1870s. A gas in a box consists of an enormous number of molecules, each with its own position and velocity. The macroscopic state, described by pressure, volume and temperature, can be realized by an astronomical number of different microscopic arrangements, called microstates. Boltzmann proposed that the entropy S is proportional to the logarithm of the number of microstates, W, consistent with a given macrostate: S equals k times the natural logarithm of W, where k is a constant now called Boltzmann's constant, approximately 1.38 times ten to the minus 23 joules per kelvin. This equation is carved on his tombstone in Vienna's Zentralfriedhof.

With this definition, the second law becomes a statement about probability. Consider a box with a partition, with all the gas on the left side. Remove the partition. The molecules spread out, not because some force pushes them, but because there are vastly more arrangements with the gas spread across both halves than with all of it on one side. For a mole of gas, about 6 times ten to the 23 molecules, the odds of all the molecules spontaneously returning to the left half are about one in ten to the power of ten to the power of 23. That is not impossible, strictly speaking, but it would not happen in many times the age of the universe.

Why, then, did the universe start with low entropy? That remains one of the deepest open questions. Our expanding universe began in a state of extraordinarily low entropy, a hot, smooth, nearly uniform plasma, and everything we observe, from the birth of stars to the metabolism of living cells, is a consequence of the universe slowly rolling downhill from that special beginning. Roger Penrose has estimated that the probability of such a low-entropy initial state, if chosen at random, is of the order of one in ten to the power of ten to the power of 123. No one knows why the universe started that way, though several proposals, involving inflation, multiverses or cyclic cosmologies, have been put forward.

Life does not violate the second law. A living organism maintains its internal order by taking in low-entropy energy, such as sunlight or chemical food, and releasing high-entropy waste heat. Erwin Schrödinger put it memorably in his 1944 lectures What Is Life?, saying that an organism feeds on "negative entropy." The Earth as a whole receives photons from the sun at a temperature of about 5,800 kelvin and radiates photons into space at about 255 kelvin. Each incoming photon is replaced by roughly twenty outgoing ones, each of lower energy. This enormous increase in the number of photons corresponds to an increase in entropy, and it is this flow that pays for the island of order we call the biosphere.

Entropy also shows up in information theory. In 1948, Claude Shannon defined the entropy of a message source as the average amount of information needed to specify an outcome, measured in bits. A fair coin has one bit of entropy per flip; a coin that lands heads 90 percent of the time has about 0.47 bits. The mathematical form is almost identical to Boltzmann's, and the parallel is not a coincidence. In 1961, Rolf Landauer showed that erasing one bit of information in a computer must dissipate at least k times T times the natural logarithm of 2 of energy as heat, about 3 times ten to the minus 21 joules at room temperature. That limit is far below what current processors use, but it has been verified experimentally in tiny systems, and it ties together computation, thermodynamics and the direction of time.

MYCORRHIZAL NETWORKS: WHAT LIES UNDER THE FOREST FLOOR

Roughly 90 percent of land plants form partnerships with fungi in their roots. The relationship, called mycorrhiza from the Greek words for fungus and root, is among the oldest and most widespread symbioses on Earth. Fossils from the Rhynie chert in Scotland, about 400 million years old, show fungal hyphae inside early land plant tissue, and some biologists argue that plants could not have colonized dry land at all without fungal help.

The basic exchange is simple. Fungal hyphae, which are threads only a few micrometers wide, spread through the soil and extract water and nutrients, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, far more efficiently than plant roots can. In return, the plant gives the fungus sugars produced by photosynthesis. Estimates suggest that a plant may allocate between 10 and 30 percent of its photosynthetic carbon to its fungal partners. Because the surface area of the fungal network is enormous, a teaspoon of healthy forest soil may contain several kilometers of hyphae.

There are two main types. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, which associate with most grasses, crops and tropical trees, penetrate the cells of the root and form branching structures called arbuscules where exchange takes place. Ectomycorrhizal fungi, common in temperate and boreal forests on pines, oaks and birches, form a sheath around the root tips and grow between cells without penetrating them. Mushrooms such as chanterelles, porcini and truffles are the fruiting bodies of ectomycorrhizal fungi, which is why they are notoriously difficult to cultivate: the fungus requires a living tree partner.

In the 1990s, the Canadian forest ecologist Suzanne Simard published experiments showing that carbon isotopes tracked in one tree could be detected in neighboring trees of different species, apparently moved through shared fungal networks. The popular press dubbed this the "wood wide web," and some accounts suggested that old "mother trees" nurture their seedlings through underground pipelines. These claims have since attracted skepticism. A 2023 review in Nature Ecology & Evolution by Justine Karst and colleagues argued that evidence for the transfer of meaningful amounts of resources between trees, and for the idea that such transfers benefit seedlings, was thinner than popular accounts suggested. Most field studies had measured the presence of fungal connections but not demonstrated a net benefit in survival or growth. Simard and others have responded with additional data and argued that the critics set the evidentiary bar unreasonably high.

What is well established is that mycorrhizal fungi strongly shape plant communities. Seedlings that grow near compatible fungal partners often do better than those that do not. Fungal diversity in the soil correlates with plant diversity. And land management matters: heavy tillage, fertilizer application and fungicides reduce mycorrhizal abundance in farmland, which may increase dependence on added fertilizer. Researchers are now experimenting with inoculating crops with selected fungal strains, with results that vary substantially with soil type and crop species.

Climate is another reason to pay attention. Soils hold about three times as much carbon as the atmosphere, and a significant share of that carbon passes through fungal tissue before being stored. Ectomycorrhizal forests tend to store more carbon in surface organic layers, while arbuscular systems tend to cycle it more quickly through the mineral soil. A 2019 study in Nature, led by Colin Averill, estimated that forests dominated by ectomycorrhizal trees held about 70 percent more carbon per unit nitrogen in their topsoil than those dominated by arbuscular partners. If shifting climate favors one type over another, the carbon balance of whole regions could shift with it.

INFLATION: WHAT IT IS AND WHAT CAUSES IT

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general level of prices, which is the same thing as a decline in the purchasing power of money. It is usually measured by tracking the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services, such as the Consumer Price Index in the United States, which the Bureau of Labor Statistics calculates monthly from prices of roughly 80,000 items in about 75 urban areas. A reading of 3 percent annual inflation means the basket costs 3 percent more than a year ago. At that rate, prices double in about 24 years, a useful shortcut being the rule of 72: divide 72 by the annual percentage rate to estimate the doubling time.

Economists distinguish several causes. Demand-pull inflation occurs when total spending in the economy grows faster than its productive capacity, so that too much money chases too few goods. Cost-push inflation arises when producers face higher input costs, for example when oil prices jump, and pass them on. A third factor is expectations: if households and firms expect prices to rise, workers demand higher wages and firms raise prices in advance, and the expectation becomes self-fulfilling. This is why central banks place such emphasis on credibility. The Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England all adopted explicit targets of about 2 percent annual inflation, partly to anchor expectations.

The monetarist view, associated with Milton Friedman, holds that inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon," meaning that it results from the money supply growing faster than output. The historical evidence is mixed but suggestive. The most extreme episodes, such as Weimar Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in 2008 and Hungary in 1946, all involved governments financing large deficits by printing money. In Hungary, the worst case on record, prices doubled roughly every 15 hours at the peak, and the country issued a banknote of 100 quintillion pengő, which is a one followed by twenty zeros. Less dramatic cases show a looser link, because the velocity at which money circulates changes, and because the definition of money is itself contestable.

The 1970s offer a different lesson. Following the 1973 oil embargo, in which Arab members of OPEC cut supplies and prices roughly quadrupled within months, many Western economies suffered "stagflation," a combination of high inflation and high unemployment that the standard Phillips curve, which posited a trade-off between the two, had not predicted. Inflation in the United States peaked at 14.8 percent in March 1980. Paul Volcker, chairman of the Federal Reserve from 1979, raised the federal funds rate to around 20 percent in 1981. The result was a deep recession, with unemployment reaching 10.8 percent in late 1982, but inflation fell to about 3.2 percent in 1983. The episode is still debated: some credit Volcker's resolve, others point to the oil glut and other factors.

More recently, inflation in 2021 and 2022 resurprised many forecasters. Supply chain disruptions from the pandemic, a surge in demand for goods as consumers shifted spending away from services, large fiscal stimulus, and then the energy shock from Russia's invasion of Ukraine pushed US inflation to 9.1 percent in June 2022, a forty-year high. Central banks responded with the fastest tightening in decades. The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate from near zero to a range of 5.25 to 5.5 percent between March 2022 and July 2023. Inflation declined substantially without the sharp rise in unemployment that many had predicted, a result economists continue to analyze.

Inflation is not equally harmful to everyone. Borrowers with fixed-rate debt benefit, because they repay in cheaper money, while savers who hold cash lose. People on fixed incomes, and those whose wages adjust slowly, bear the largest burden. Because poor households spend a larger share of their income on necessities such as food and energy, whose prices often move faster than the average, the burden often falls disproportionately on them. At the same time, mild inflation is thought by many economists to be useful, because it allows real wages to adjust downward in a recession without requiring painful nominal wage cuts, which workers strongly resist. Deflation, by contrast, can be dangerous, as Japan's experience from the 1990s showed: falling prices encourage consumers to delay purchases and increase the real burden of debts.

THE TIDES: WHY THE OCEAN RISES TWICE A DAY

Most people know that the moon causes the tides, but the usual explanation, that the moon's gravity pulls the water toward it, accounts for only half the story. If that were the whole truth, there would be one high tide a day, on the side of the Earth facing the moon. In fact, most coastlines experience two high tides roughly every 24 hours and 50 minutes.

The answer lies in the fact that gravity weakens with distance. The moon pulls on the near side of the Earth more strongly than on the center and pulls on the center more strongly than on the far side. The difference between these pulls, called the tidal force, stretches the Earth along the line toward the moon. Relative to the center of the Earth, the water on the near side is pulled toward the moon, while the water on the far side is, in effect, left behind. The result is two bulges, one facing the moon and one on the opposite side. As the Earth rotates once beneath this pattern every 24 hours, an observer at a fixed point passes through both bulges. The extra 50 minutes arises because the moon is itself moving along its orbit in the same direction as the Earth's rotation, so the Earth must turn a little farther each day to catch up.

The sun also contributes. Although it is about 27 million times more massive than the moon, it is about 390 times farther away, and since the tidal force falls off with the cube of distance, the sun's tidal effect is about 46 percent of the moon's. When the sun, Earth and moon line up at new moon and full moon, the two effects add up, producing larger than usual spring tides. At the first and third quarters, when the sun and moon pull at right angles, the effects partly cancel, giving smaller neap tides. The word spring here has nothing to do with the season; it comes from an older sense of the word meaning to leap up.

Real tides are far more complicated than this idealized picture, because the oceans are not a uniform layer of water covering a smooth planet. Continents block the flow, basins have their own natural resonant periods, and the Coriolis effect swirls the tide into rotating patterns around amphidromic points, locations where the tidal range is nearly zero. The Bay of Fundy in eastern Canada has the largest tidal range in the world, up to about 16 meters between low and high water, because its funnel shape and length produce a resonance matching the period of the semi-diurnal tide. In the Mediterranean, by contrast, the range is usually only a few tens of centimeters.

Tides also act on the Earth and moon themselves. Friction between the ocean and the sea floor drags the tidal bulge slightly ahead of the line between Earth and moon, and the bulge's gravity pulls the moon forward in its orbit. This transfers angular momentum from the Earth to the moon, which is slowly spiraling away at about 3.8 centimeters per year, a figure measured by bouncing lasers off reflectors left on the lunar surface by Apollo astronauts and Soviet landers. At the same time, the Earth's rotation is slowing. Growth bands in fossil corals from the Devonian period, about 400 million years ago, suggest that a year then contained roughly 400 days. Eventually, in the very distant future, the Earth's rotation could synchronize with the moon's orbit, though the sun will probably evolve before that happens.

THE MEKONG RIVER: A BASIN OF SIX COUNTRIES

The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau at an altitude of more than 5,000 meters and flows about 4,350 kilometers through or along the borders of China, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam before emptying into the South China Sea. It is the twelfth longest river in the world and carries a mean annual discharge of roughly 475 cubic kilometers, which places it among the ten largest by volume. About 60 million people live in the lower basin, and the river supports one of the most productive inland fisheries on Earth, with an estimated annual catch of two to three million tonnes.

Its hydrology is dominated by the monsoon. Between May and October, heavy rain and snowmelt swell the river, and the flow at Phnom Penh may rise to more than 40,000 cubic meters per second. At this point something unusual happens. The Mekong's main channel meets the Tonlé Sap River, which in the dry season drains the great Tonlé Sap lake in Cambodia toward the Mekong. When the flood arrives, the pressure of the Mekong's water reverses the flow of the Tonlé Sap River, pushing water back up into the lake. The lake, which shrinks to about 2,500 square kilometers in the dry season, expands to as much as 15,000 square kilometers, flooding vast forests and providing breeding habitat for fish. When the flood recedes, the flow reverses again. The reversal, which occurs around October, is celebrated in Cambodia at the Bon Om Touk water festival, which attracts millions of visitors to Phnom Penh each year.

The river also builds land. Over thousands of years, sediment has formed the Mekong Delta in southern Vietnam, a flat, low-lying area of about 40,000 square kilometers that produces roughly half of the country's rice and a large share of its exported seafood. The delta is, however, under pressure. Dams upstream, including a cascade of eleven large hydropower dams on the Chinese section known as the Lancang and a growing number in Laos and Cambodia, trap sediment, reducing the amount that reaches the delta. Sand mining in the river bed has also removed substantial quantities. Combined with groundwater pumping that causes the land to sink at several centimeters a year, and rising sea level, the delta is losing ground, and some projections suggest that a large portion could be inundated by the end of the century under high emissions scenarios.

Governance of the river is complicated. In 1995 the four lower-basin countries, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam, established the Mekong River Commission to coordinate management. China and Myanmar participate only as dialogue partners, which limits the body's reach over upstream dams. In 2019 and 2020, during a severe drought, downstream communities complained that water levels were abnormally low even as upstream reservoirs filled, sparking debate over data sharing. Studies by independent researchers suggested that the dams held back large volumes during a period when the rest of the basin was short of water, though the interpretation of the evidence remains contested.

The fishery is a particularly important case. The migratory fish of the Mekong, including the giant Mekong catfish, which can grow to over three meters long and weigh more than 250 kilograms, travel hundreds of kilometers upstream to spawn and downstream to feed. Dams interrupt these movements, and fish ladders designed for European salmon often work poorly for tropical species. Conservation groups warn that without careful planning, one of the most productive freshwater ecosystems on the planet may be significantly diminished within a generation.

          method: "POST",
          body: JSON.stringify({ title }),
        });
        dispatch({ type: "added", task: created });
      } catch (err) {
        setError((err as Error).message);
        setDraft(title);
      }
    },
    [draft, projectId]
  );

  const move = async (id: string, to: Status) => {
    dispatch({ type: "moved", id, to }); // optimistic
    try {
      await api<void>(`/tasks/${id}`, { method: "PATCH", body: JSON.stringify({ status: to }) });
    } catch (err) {
      setError((err as Error).message);
      // reload to resync with the server after a failed optimistic update
      const fresh = await api<Task[]>(`/projects/${projectId}/tasks`);
      dispatch({ type: "loaded", tasks: fresh });
    }
  };

  if (loading) return <p role="status">Loading tasks...</p>;

  return (
    <section className="board">
      <form onSubmit={addTask} className="board__form">
        <input
          value={draft}
          onChange={(e) => setDraft(e.target.value)}
          placeholder="Add a task and press Enter"
          aria-label="New task title"
        />
        <input
          value={filter}
          onChange={(e) => setFilter(e.target.value)}
          placeholder="Filter"
          aria-label="Filter tasks"
        />
      </form>
      {error && (
        <div className="board__error" role="alert">
          {error} <button onClick={() => setError(null)}>Dismiss</button>
        </div>
      )}
      <div className="board__columns">
        {COLUMNS.map((col) => {
          const items = visible.filter((t) => t.status === col.key);
          return (
            <div key={col.key} className="column">
              <h3>
                {col.label} <span className="count">{items.length}</span>
              </h3>
              <ul>
                {items.map((t) => (
                  <li key={t.id} className="card">
                    <strong>{t.title}</strong>
                    {t.assignee && <em> @{t.assignee}</em>}
                    <div className="card__actions">
                      {COLUMNS.filter((c) => c.key !== t.status).map((c) => (
                        <button key={c.key} onClick={() => move(t.id, c.key)}>
                          to {c.label.toLowerCase()}
                        </button>
                      ))}
                      <button onClick={() => dispatch({ type: "removed", id: t.id })}>delete</button>
                    </div>
                  </li>
                ))}
              </ul>
            </div>
          );
        })}
      </div>
    </section>
  );
}

function requireUser(req, _res, next) {
  const header = req.get("authorization") || "";
  const [scheme, token] = header.split(" ");
  if (scheme !== "Bearer" || !token) return next(new HttpError(401, "missing bearer token"));
  const expected = crypto.createHmac("sha256", process.env.TOKEN_SECRET).update("demo-user").digest("hex");
  const a = Buffer.from(token);
  const b = Buffer.from(expected);
  if (a.length !== b.length || !crypto.timingSafeEqual(a, b)) {
    return next(new HttpError(403, "invalid token"));
  }
  req.user = { id: "demo-user" };
  next();
}

export function debounce<A extends unknown[]>(fn: (...args: A) => void, waitMs: number) {
  let timer: ReturnType<typeof setTimeout> | undefined;
  const debounced = (...args: A) => {
    if (timer) clearTimeout(timer);
    timer = setTimeout(() => {
      timer = undefined;
      fn(...args);
    }, waitMs);
  };
  debounced.cancel = () => {
    if (timer) clearTimeout(timer);
    timer = undefined;
  };
  return debounced;
}

export function groupBy<T, K extends string | number>(items: readonly T[], key: (t: T) => K): Record<K, T[]> {
  const out = {} as Record<K, T[]>;
  for (const item of items) {
    const k = key(item);
    (out[k] ||= []).push(item);
  }
  return out;
}

use std::collections::HashMap;
use std::fmt;
use std::time::{Duration, Instant};

/// Errors that can occur while consuming tokens.
#[derive(Debug, PartialEq)]
pub enum RateError {
    /// The caller must wait this long before trying again.
    Limited { retry_after: Duration },
    /// The request asked for more tokens than the bucket can ever hold.
    TooLarge { requested: u32, capacity: u32 },
}

impl fmt::Display for RateError {
    fn fmt(&self, f: &mut fmt::Formatter<'_>) -> fmt::Result {
        match self {
            RateError::Limited { retry_after } => {
                write!(f, "rate limited, retry in {:.2}s", retry_after.as_secs_f64())
            }
            RateError::TooLarge { requested, capacity } => {
                write!(f, "requested {requested} tokens but capacity is {capacity}")
            }
        }
    }
}

impl std::error::Error for RateError {}

#[derive(Debug, Clone)]
pub struct Bucket {
    capacity: u32,
    refill_per_sec: f64,
    tokens: f64,
    last: Instant,
}

impl Bucket {
    pub fn new(capacity: u32, refill_per_sec: f64, now: Instant) -> Self {
        Self { capacity, refill_per_sec, tokens: capacity as f64, last: now }
    }

    fn refill(&mut self, now: Instant) {
        let elapsed = now.saturating_duration_since(self.last).as_secs_f64();
        self.tokens = (self.tokens + elapsed * self.refill_per_sec).min(self.capacity as f64);
        self.last = now;
    }

    pub fn try_take(&mut self, n: u32, now: Instant) -> Result<(), RateError> {
        if n > self.capacity {
            return Err(RateError::TooLarge { requested: n, capacity: self.capacity });
        }
        self.refill(now);
        let need = n as f64;
        if self.tokens >= need {
            self.tokens -= need;
            Ok(())
        } else {
            let deficit = need - self.tokens;
            let secs = deficit / self.refill_per_sec;
            Err(RateError::Limited { retry_after: Duration::from_secs_f64(secs) })
        }
    }
}

/// A keyed set of buckets, e.g. one per client IP.
pub struct Limiter {
    capacity: u32,
    refill_per_sec: f64,
    buckets: HashMap<String, Bucket>,
}

impl Limiter {
    pub fn new(capacity: u32, refill_per_sec: f64) -> Self {
        Self { capacity, refill_per_sec, buckets: HashMap::new() }
    }

    pub fn check(&mut self, key: &str, cost: u32) -> Result<(), RateError> {
        let now = Instant::now();
        let (cap, rate) = (self.capacity, self.refill_per_sec);
        self.buckets
            .entry(key.to_owned())
            .or_insert_with(|| Bucket::new(cap, rate, now))
            .try_take(cost, now)
    }

    /// Drop buckets that have been idle long enough to be full again.
    pub fn sweep(&mut self, idle: Duration) {
        let now = Instant::now();
        self.buckets.retain(|_, b| now.saturating_duration_since(b.last) < idle);
    }
}

use serde::Deserialize;
use std::{fs, io, path::Path};
use thiserror::Error;

#[derive(Debug, Error)]
pub enum ConfigError {
    #[error("could not read {path}: {source}")]
    Read { path: String, source: io::Error },
    #[error("invalid TOML in {path}: {source}")]
    Parse { path: String, source: toml::de::Error },
    #[error("invalid value: {0}")]
    Invalid(&'static str),
}

#[derive(Debug, Deserialize, Clone)]
pub struct Config {
    pub listen: String,
    #[serde(default = "default_workers")]
    pub workers: usize,
    pub storage: Storage,
    #[serde(default)]
    pub log_level: LogLevel,
}

#[derive(Debug, Deserialize, Clone)]
pub struct Storage {
    pub path: String,
    #[serde(default = "default_cache_mb")]
    pub cache_mb: u64,
}

#[derive(Debug, Deserialize, Clone, Copy, Default, PartialEq, Eq)]
#[serde(rename_all = "lowercase")]
pub enum LogLevel {
    Debug,
    #[default]
    Info,
    Warn,
    Error,
}

fn default_workers() -> usize {
    std::thread::available_parallelism().map(|n| n.get()).unwrap_or(2)
}

fn default_cache_mb() -> u64 {
    256
}

pub trait Validate {
    fn validate(&self) -> Result<(), ConfigError>;
}

impl Validate for Config {
    fn validate(&self) -> Result<(), ConfigError> {
        if self.workers == 0 || self.workers > 512 {
            return Err(ConfigError::Invalid("workers must be between 1 and 512"));
        }
        if !self.listen.contains(':') {
            return Err(ConfigError::Invalid("listen must look like host:port"));
        }
        if self.storage.cache_mb < 8 {
            return Err(ConfigError::Invalid("cache_mb below 8 is not supported"));
        }
        Ok(())
    }
}

pub fn load(path: impl AsRef<Path>) -> Result<Config, ConfigError> {
    let path = path.as_ref();
    let display = path.display().to_string();
    let text = fs::read_to_string(path).map_err(|source| ConfigError::Read { path: display.clone(), source })?;
    let cfg: Config = toml::from_str(&text).map_err(|source| ConfigError::Parse { path: display, source })?;
    cfg.validate()?;
    Ok(cfg)
}

use std::collections::BTreeMap;

#[derive(Debug, Clone, PartialEq)]
pub struct Item {
    pub sku: String,
    pub name: String,
    pub qty: u32,
    pub price_cents: u64,
}

pub trait Store {
    fn get(&self, sku: &str) -> Option<&Item>;
    fn put(&mut self, item: Item) -> Option<Item>;
    fn remove(&mut self, sku: &str) -> Option<Item>;
    fn total_value_cents(&self) -> u64;
}

#[derive(Default)]
pub struct MemoryStore {
    items: BTreeMap<String, Item>,
}

impl Store for MemoryStore {
    fn get(&self, sku: &str) -> Option<&Item> {
        self.items.get(sku)
    }

    fn put(&mut self, item: Item) -> Option<Item> {
        self.items.insert(item.sku.clone(), item)
    }

    fn remove(&mut self, sku: &str) -> Option<Item> {
        self.items.remove(sku)
    }

    fn total_value_cents(&self) -> u64 {
        self.items.values().map(|i| i.qty as u64 * i.price_cents).sum()
    }
}

pub fn restock<S: Store>(store: &mut S, sku: &str, extra: u32) -> Result<u32, String> {
    let current = store.get(sku).cloned().ok_or_else(|| format!("unknown sku {sku}"))?;
    let qty = current.qty.checked_add(extra).ok_or("quantity overflow")?;
    store.put(Item { qty, ..current });
    Ok(qty)
}

package main

import (
	"context"
	"encoding/json"
	"errors"
	"flag"
	"fmt"
	"io"
	"log"
	"net/http"
	"os"
	"sync"
	"time"
)

type Result struct {
	URL     string        `json:"url"`
	Status  int           `json:"status"`
	Bytes   int64         `json:"bytes"`
	Elapsed time.Duration `json:"elapsed_ns"`
	Err     string        `json:"error,omitempty"`
}

func fetch(ctx context.Context, client *http.Client, url string) Result {
	start := time.Now()
	r := Result{URL: url}
	req, err := http.NewRequestWithContext(ctx, http.MethodGet, url, nil)
	if err != nil {
		r.Err = err.Error()
		return r
	}
	resp, err := client.Do(req)
	if err != nil {
		r.Err = err.Error()
		r.Elapsed = time.Since(start)
		return r
	}
	defer resp.Body.Close()
	n, err := io.Copy(io.Discard, io.LimitReader(resp.Body, 10<<20))
	if err != nil && !errors.Is(err, context.DeadlineExceeded) {
		r.Err = err.Error()
	}
	r.Status = resp.StatusCode
	r.Bytes = n
	r.Elapsed = time.Since(start)
	return r
}

func worker(ctx context.Context, client *http.Client, jobs <-chan string, out chan<- Result, wg *sync.WaitGroup) {
	defer wg.Done()
	for u := range jobs {
		out <- fetch(ctx, client, u)
	}
}

func main() {
	var (
		concurrency = flag.Int("c", 4, "number of concurrent workers")
		timeout     = flag.Duration("timeout", 8*time.Second, "per-request timeout")
	)
	flag.Parse()
	urls := flag.Args()
	if len(urls) == 0 {
		fmt.Fprintln(os.Stderr, "usage: fetcher [-c N] [-timeout D] URL...")
		os.Exit(2)
	}

	ctx, cancel := context.WithCancel(context.Background())
	defer cancel()
	client := &http.Client{Timeout: *timeout}

	jobs := make(chan string)
	results := make(chan Result)
	var wg sync.WaitGroup
	for i := 0; i < *concurrency; i++ {
		wg.Add(1)
		go worker(ctx, client, jobs, results, &wg)
	}

	go func() {
		for _, u := range urls {
			jobs <- u
		}
		close(jobs)
		wg.Wait()
		close(results)
	}()

	enc := json.NewEncoder(os.Stdout)
	failed := 0
	for r := range results {
		if r.Err != "" || r.Status >= 400 {
			failed++
		}
		if err := enc.Encode(r); err != nil {
			log.Fatalf("encode: %v", err)
		}
	}
	if failed > 0 {
		log.Printf("%d of %d requests failed", failed, len(urls))
		os.Exit(1)
	}
}

package cache

import "container/list"

type entry[K comparable, V any] struct {
	key K
	val V
}

// LRU is a small, non-thread-safe least recently used cache.
type LRU[K comparable, V any] struct {
	cap   int
	order *list.List
	items map[K]*list.Element
}

func NewLRU[K comparable, V any](capacity int) *LRU[K, V] {
	return &LRU[K, V]{cap: capacity, order: list.New(), items: make(map[K]*list.Element)}
}

func (c *LRU[K, V]) Get(k K) (V, bool) {
	if el, ok := c.items[k]; ok {
		c.order.MoveToFront(el)
		return el.Value.(*entry[K, V]).val, true
	}
	var zero V
	return zero, false
}

func (c *LRU[K, V]) Put(k K, v V) {
	if el, ok := c.items[k]; ok {
		el.Value.(*entry[K, V]).val = v
		c.order.MoveToFront(el)
		return
	}
	c.items[k] = c.order.PushFront(&entry[K, V]{k, v})
	if c.order.Len() > c.cap {
		last := c.order.Back()
		c.order.Remove(last)
		delete(c.items, last.Value.(*entry[K, V]).key)
	}
}

-- Open tasks per assignee, busiest first.
SELECT u.display_name,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE t.status = 'todo')  AS todo,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE t.status = 'doing') AS doing,
       count(*)                                    AS open_total
  FROM tasks t
  JOIN users u ON u.id = t.assignee_id
 WHERE t.status <> 'done'
 GROUP BY u.id, u.display_name
 ORDER BY open_total DESC, u.display_name;

-- Weekly throughput for the last 12 weeks, using a window function for the running total.
WITH weekly AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('week', t.created_at) AS week,
           count(*) AS created
      FROM tasks t
     WHERE t.created_at >= now() - interval '12 weeks'
     GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT week::date,
       created,
       sum(created) OVER (ORDER BY week) AS running_total,
       round(avg(created) OVER (ORDER BY week ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW), 1) AS moving_avg
  FROM weekly
 ORDER BY week;

-- Overdue tasks that nobody owns.
SELECT t.id, t.title, p.name AS project, now() - t.due_at AS overdue_by
  FROM tasks t
  JOIN projects p ON p.id = t.project_id
 WHERE t.due_at < now()
   AND t.status <> 'done'
   AND t.assignee_id IS NULL
   AND NOT p.archived
 ORDER BY t.due_at;

import { useCallback, useEffect, useRef, useState } from "react";

export function useLocalStorage<T>(key: string, initial: T): [T, (v: T | ((prev: T) => T)) => void] {
  const [value, setValue] = useState<T>(() => {
    try {
      const raw = window.localStorage.getItem(key);
      return raw === null ? initial : (JSON.parse(raw) as T);
    } catch {
      return initial;
    }
  });
  const latest = useRef(value);
  latest.current = value;

  const set = useCallback(
    (next: T | ((prev: T) => T)) => {
      const resolved = typeof next === "function" ? (next as (p: T) => T)(latest.current) : next;
      setValue(resolved);
      try {
        window.localStorage.setItem(key, JSON.stringify(resolved));
      } catch {
        /* quota exceeded or storage disabled; keep the in-memory value */
      }
    },
    [key]
  );

  // Keep multiple tabs in sync.
  useEffect(() => {
    const onStorage = (e: StorageEvent) => {
      if (e.key === key && e.newValue !== null) {
        try {
          setValue(JSON.parse(e.newValue) as T);
        } catch {
          /* ignore malformed data */
        }
      }
    };
    window.addEventListener("storage", onStorage);
    return () => window.removeEventListener("storage", onStorage);
  }, [key]);

  return [value, set];
}

use std::collections::HashMap;
use std::sync::{Arc, Mutex};

type Handler<E> = Box<dyn Fn(&E) + Send + Sync + 'static>;

pub struct Emitter<E> {
    next_id: Mutex<u64>,
    handlers: Arc<Mutex<HashMap<u64, Handler<E>>>>,
}

pub struct Subscription<E> {
    id: u64,
    handlers: Arc<Mutex<HashMap<u64, Handler<E>>>>,
}

impl<E> Drop for Subscription<E> {
    fn drop(&mut self) {
        if let Ok(mut map) = self.handlers.lock() {
            map.remove(&self.id);
        }
    }
}

impl<E> Emitter<E> {
    pub fn new() -> Self {
        Self { next_id: Mutex::new(0), handlers: Arc::new(Mutex::new(HashMap::new())) }
    }

    pub fn subscribe<F>(&self, f: F) -> Subscription<E>
    where
        F: Fn(&E) + Send + Sync + 'static,
    {
        let mut next = self.next_id.lock().unwrap();
        let id = *next;
        *next += 1;
        self.handlers.lock().unwrap().insert(id, Box::new(f));
        Subscription { id, handlers: Arc::clone(&self.handlers) }
    }

    pub fn emit(&self, event: &E) {
        let map = self.handlers.lock().unwrap();
        for handler in map.values() {
            handler(event);
        }
    }
}

    fn from(e: ParseIntError) -> Self {
        VersionError::BadNumber(e.to_string())
    }

impl FromStr for Version {
    type Err = VersionError;

    fn from_str(s: &str) -> Result<Self, Self::Err> {
        let parts: Vec<&str> = s.trim_start_matches('v').split('.').collect();
        if parts.len() != 3 {
            return Err(VersionError::WrongPartCount(parts.len()));
        }
        Ok(Version { major: parts[0].parse()?, minor: parts[1].parse()?, patch: parts[2].parse()? })
    }
}

    fn partial_cmp(&self, other: &Self) -> Option<std::cmp::Ordering> {
        Some(self.cmp(other))
    }
}

impl Ord for Version {
    fn cmp(&self, other: &Self) -> std::cmp::Ordering {
        (self.major, self.minor, self.patch).cmp(&(other.major, other.minor, other.patch))
    }

package retry

import (
	"context"
	"math/rand"
	"time"
)

// Do calls fn until it succeeds, the attempts run out, or ctx is cancelled.
// Delays grow exponentially with full jitter.
func Do(ctx context.Context, attempts int, base, max time.Duration, fn func() error) error {
	var err error
	for i := 0; i < attempts; i++ {
		if err = fn(); err == nil {
			return nil
		}
		backoff := base << uint(i)
		if backoff > max || backoff <= 0 {
			backoff = max
		}
		sleep := time.Duration(rand.Int63n(int64(backoff) + 1))
		select {
		case <-ctx.Done():
			return ctx.Err()
		case <-time.After(sleep):
		}
	}
	return err
}

# taskboard

A small kanban-style task tracker with a React front end and an Express and Postgres back end.

## Quick start

```bash
docker compose up --build
open http://localhost:3000
```

For local development without Docker you need Node 20 or newer and a running Postgres.

```bash
npm ci
createdb taskboard && psql taskboard -f db/schema.sql
export DATABASE_URL=postgres://localhost/taskboard TOKEN_SECRET=dev
npm run dev
```

## Project layout

| Path            | What lives there                              |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| `server/`       | Express API, one file per resource            |
| `src/`          | React application and shared hooks            |
| `db/`           | Schema, reports, and numbered migrations      |
| `deploy/k8s/`   | Kubernetes manifests                          |

## API

All endpoints expect an `Authorization: Bearer <token>` header.

- `GET /api/projects/:id/tasks` returns every task in a project, newest first.
- `POST /api/projects/:id/tasks` creates a task. Body: `{ "title": "..." }`.
- `PATCH /api/tasks/:id` updates the status. Body: `{ "status": "todo" | "doing" | "done" }`.

Errors are JSON of the form `{ "error": "message" }`. Server faults never leak internals.

## Testing

`npm test` runs the unit tests with Vitest. Integration tests need `DATABASE_URL` and will
skip themselves with a clear message when it is not set.

const UNITS = ["B", "KB", "MB", "GB", "TB"] as const;

export function formatBytes(n: number, digits = 1): string {
  if (!Number.isFinite(n) || n < 0) return "n/a";
  let i = 0;
  let v = n;
  while (v >= 1024 && i < UNITS.length - 1) {
    v /= 1024;
    i++;
  }
  return `${i === 0 ? v : v.toFixed(digits)} ${UNITS[i]}`;
}

export function relativeTime(then: number, now = Date.now()): string {
  const diff = Math.round((then - now) / 1000);
  const rtf = new Intl.RelativeTimeFormat("en", { numeric: "auto" });
  const steps: [Intl.RelativeTimeFormatUnit, number][] = [
    ["year", 31536000],
    ["month", 2592000],
    ["day", 86400],
    ["hour", 3600],
    ["minute", 60],
  ];
  for (const [unit, secs] of steps) {
    if (Math.abs(diff) >= secs) return rtf.format(Math.round(diff / secs), unit);
  }
  return rtf.format(diff, "second");
}

export function slugify(input: string): string {
  return input
    .normalize("NFKD")
    .replace(/[̀-ͯ]/g, "")
    .toLowerCase()
    .replace(/[^a-z0-9]+/g, "-")
    .replace(/^-+|-+$/g, "")
    .slice(0, 60);
}

export class AsyncQueue<T> {
  private items: T[] = [];
  private waiters: Array<(value: T) => void> = [];

  push(item: T): void {
    const waiter = this.waiters.shift();
    if (waiter) waiter(item);
    else this.items.push(item);
  }

  pop(): Promise<T> {
    const ready = this.items.shift();
    if (ready !== undefined) return Promise.resolve(ready);
    return new Promise<T>((resolve) => this.waiters.push(resolve));
  }

  get size(): number {
    return this.items.length;
  }
}

export async function mapLimit<T, R>(
  items: readonly T[],
  limit: number,
  fn: (item: T, index: number) => Promise<R>
): Promise<R[]> {
  const results = new Array<R>(items.length);
  let next = 0;
  async function run() {
    while (true) {
      const i = next++;
      if (i >= items.length) return;
      results[i] = await fn(items[i] as T, i);
    }
  }
  await Promise.all(Array.from({ length: Math.min(limit, items.length) }, run));
  return results;
}

function cors(allowed) {
  const set = new Set(allowed);
  return function corsMiddleware(req, res, next) {
    const origin = req.get("origin");
    if (origin && set.has(origin)) {
      res.set("Access-Control-Allow-Origin", origin);
      res.set("Vary", "Origin");
      res.set("Access-Control-Allow-Headers", "Authorization, Content-Type");
      res.set("Access-Control-Allow-Methods", "GET, POST, PATCH, DELETE");
    }
    if (req.method === "OPTIONS") return res.sendStatus(204);
    next();
  };
}

func (s *Server) Routes() http.Handler {
	mux := http.NewServeMux()
	mux.HandleFunc("GET /notes", s.listNotes)
	mux.HandleFunc("POST /notes", s.createNote)
	mux.HandleFunc("DELETE /notes/{id}", s.deleteNote)

}

func (s *Server) logging(next http.Handler) http.Handler {
	return http.HandlerFunc(func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
		start := time.Now()
		next.ServeHTTP(w, r)
		s.Logger.Info("request", "method", r.Method, "path", r.URL.Path, "dur", time.Since(start))
	})
}

func writeJSON(w http.ResponseWriter, status int, v any) {
	w.Header().Set("Content-Type", "application/json")
	w.WriteHeader(status)
	_ = json.NewEncoder(w).Encode(v)
}

func (s *Server) listNotes(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
	limit := 50
	if q := r.URL.Query().Get("limit"); q != "" {
		n, err := strconv.Atoi(q)
		if err != nil || n < 1 || n > 500 {
			writeJSON(w, http.StatusBadRequest, map[string]string{"error": "limit must be 1..500"})
			return
		}
		limit = n
	}
	rows, err := s.DB.QueryContext(r.Context(),
		`SELECT id, body, pinned, created_at FROM notes ORDER BY pinned DESC, created_at DESC LIMIT $1`, limit)
	if err != nil {
		s.Logger.Error("query failed", "err", err)
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusInternalServerError, map[string]string{"error": "internal error"})
		return
	}
	defer rows.Close()
	notes := make([]Note, 0, limit)
	for rows.Next() {
		var n Note
		if err := rows.Scan(&n.ID, &n.Body, &n.Pinned, &n.CreatedAt); err != nil {
			s.Logger.Error("scan failed", "err", err)
			writeJSON(w, http.StatusInternalServerError, map[string]string{"error": "internal error"})
			return
		}
		notes = append(notes, n)
	}
	writeJSON(w, http.StatusOK, notes)
}

func (s *Server) createNote(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
	var in struct {
		Body   string `json:"body"`
		Pinned bool   `json:"pinned"`
	}
	dec := json.NewDecoder(http.MaxBytesReader(w, r.Body, 1<<16))
	dec.DisallowUnknownFields()
	if err := dec.Decode(&in); err != nil {
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusBadRequest, map[string]string{"error": "invalid JSON: " + err.Error()})
		return
	}
	in.Body = strings.TrimSpace(in.Body)
	if in.Body == "" {
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusBadRequest, map[string]string{"error": "body is required"})
		return
	}
	var n Note
	err := s.DB.QueryRowContext(r.Context(),
		`INSERT INTO notes (body, pinned) VALUES ($1, $2) RETURNING id, body, pinned, created_at`,
		in.Body, in.Pinned).Scan(&n.ID, &n.Body, &n.Pinned, &n.CreatedAt)
	if err != nil {
		s.Logger.Error("insert failed", "err", err)
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusInternalServerError, map[string]string{"error": "internal error"})
		return
	}
	writeJSON(w, http.StatusCreated, n)
}

func (s *Server) deleteNote(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
	id, err := strconv.ParseInt(r.PathValue("id"), 10, 64)
	if err != nil {
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusBadRequest, map[string]string{"error": "bad id"})
		return
	}
	res, err := s.DB.ExecContext(r.Context(), `DELETE FROM notes WHERE id = $1`, id)
	if err != nil {
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusInternalServerError, map[string]string{"error": "internal error"})
		return
	}
	if n, _ := res.RowsAffected(); n == 0 {
		writeJSON(w, http.StatusNotFound, map[string]string{"error": errors.New("no such note").Error()})
		return
	}
	w.WriteHeader(http.StatusNoContent)
}

use std::ops::{Add, Index, IndexMut, Mul};

#[derive(Debug, Clone, PartialEq)]
pub struct Matrix {
    rows: usize,
    cols: usize,
    data: Vec<f64>,
}

impl Matrix {
    pub fn zeros(rows: usize, cols: usize) -> Self {
        Self { rows, cols, data: vec![0.0; rows * cols] }
    }

    pub fn identity(n: usize) -> Self {
        let mut m = Self::zeros(n, n);
        for i in 0..n {
            m[(i, i)] = 1.0;
        }
        m
    }

    pub fn from_rows(rows: &[&[f64]]) -> Option<Self> {
        let cols = rows.first()?.len();
        if rows.iter().any(|r| r.len() != cols) {
            return None;
        }
        let data = rows.iter().flat_map(|r| r.iter().copied()).collect();
        Some(Self { rows: rows.len(), cols, data })
    }

    pub fn transpose(&self) -> Matrix {
        let mut t = Matrix::zeros(self.cols, self.rows);
        for r in 0..self.rows {
            for c in 0..self.cols {
                t[(c, r)] = self[(r, c)];
            }
        }
        t
    }
}

impl Index<(usize, usize)> for Matrix {
    type Output = f64;
    fn index(&self, (r, c): (usize, usize)) -> &f64 {
        &self.data[r * self.cols + c]
    }
}

impl IndexMut<(usize, usize)> for Matrix {
    fn index_mut(&mut self, (r, c): (usize, usize)) -> &mut f64 {
        &mut self.data[r * self.cols + c]
    }
}

impl<'a> Add for &'a Matrix {
    type Output = Matrix;
    fn add(self, rhs: &Matrix) -> Matrix {
        assert_eq!((self.rows, self.cols), (rhs.rows, rhs.cols), "shape mismatch");
        let data = self.data.iter().zip(&rhs.data).map(|(a, b)| a + b).collect();
        Matrix { rows: self.rows, cols: self.cols, data }
    }
}

impl<'a> Mul for &'a Matrix {
    type Output = Matrix;
    fn mul(self, rhs: &Matrix) -> Matrix {
        assert_eq!(self.cols, rhs.rows, "inner dimensions must agree");
        let mut out = Matrix::zeros(self.rows, rhs.cols);
        for i in 0..self.rows {
            for k in 0..self.cols {
                let a = self[(i, k)];
                for j in 0..rhs.cols {
                    out[(i, j)] += a * rhs[(k, j)];
                }
            }
        }
        out
    }
}

use std::collections::HashMap;
use std::env;
use std::fs::File;
use std::io::{self, BufRead, BufReader};
use std::process::ExitCode;

fn count_words<R: BufRead>(reader: R) -> io::Result<HashMap<String, usize>> {
    let mut counts = HashMap::new();
    for line in reader.lines() {
        let line = line?;
        for word in line
            .split(|c: char| !c.is_alphanumeric() && c != '\'')
            .filter(|w| !w.is_empty())
        {
            *counts.entry(word.to_lowercase()).or_insert(0) += 1;
        }
    }
    Ok(counts)
}

fn main() -> ExitCode {
    let Some(path) = env::args().nth(1) else {
        eprintln!("usage: wordcount <file>");
        return ExitCode::from(2);
    };
    let file = match File::open(&path) {
        Ok(f) => f,
        Err(e) => {
            eprintln!("cannot open {path}: {e}");
            return ExitCode::FAILURE;
        }
    };
    let counts = match count_words(BufReader::new(file)) {
        Ok(c) => c,
        Err(e) => {
            eprintln!("read error: {e}");
            return ExitCode::FAILURE;
        }
    };
    let mut entries: Vec<_> = counts.into_iter().collect();
    entries.sort_by(|a, b| b.1.cmp(&a.1).then_with(|| a.0.cmp(&b.0)));
    for (word, n) in entries.into_iter().take(20) {
        println!("{n:>6}  {word}");
    }
    ExitCode::SUCCESS
}

-- Cohort retention: share of users still active N weeks after signup.
WITH cohorts AS (
    SELECT id AS user_id, date_trunc('week', created_at)::date AS cohort_week
      FROM users
),
activity AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT t.assignee_id AS user_id, date_trunc('week', t.created_at)::date AS active_week
      FROM tasks t
     WHERE t.assignee_id IS NOT NULL
)
SELECT c.cohort_week,
       ((a.active_week - c.cohort_week) / 7) AS weeks_since_signup,
       count(DISTINCT a.user_id) AS active_users,
       round(100.0 * count(DISTINCT a.user_id)
             / sum(count(DISTINCT a.user_id)) FILTER (WHERE a.active_week = c.cohort_week)
               OVER (PARTITION BY c.cohort_week), 1) AS pct_of_week_zero
  FROM cohorts c
  JOIN activity a ON a.user_id = c.user_id AND a.active_week >= c.cohort_week
 GROUP BY c.cohort_week, weeks_since_signup
 ORDER BY c.cohort_week, weeks_since_signup;

type Toast = { id: number; message: string; kind: "info" | "error" };

const ToastContext = createContext<{ push: (message: string, kind?: Toast["kind"]) => void } | null>(null);

export function ToastProvider({ children }: { children: ReactNode }) {
  const [toasts, setToasts] = useState<Toast[]>([]);
  const nextId = useRef(1);

  const push = useCallback((message: string, kind: Toast["kind"] = "info") => {
    const id = nextId.current++;
    setToasts((t) => [...t, { id, message, kind }]);
    setTimeout(() => setToasts((t) => t.filter((x) => x.id !== id)), 4000);
  }, []);

  return (
    <ToastContext.Provider value={{ push }}>
      {children}
      <div className="toasts" aria-live="polite">
        {toasts.map((t) => (
          <div key={t.id} className={`toast toast--${t.kind}`}>
            {t.message}
          </div>
        ))}
      </div>
    </ToastContext.Provider>
  );
}

export function useToast() {
  const ctx = useContext(ToastContext);
  if (!ctx) throw new Error("useToast must be used inside <ToastProvider>");
  return ctx;
}

package stats

import (
	"math"
	"sort"
)

// Percentile returns the p-th percentile (0..100) using linear interpolation
// between closest ranks. It does not modify the input slice.
func Percentile(values []float64, p float64) float64 {
	if len(values) == 0 {
		return math.NaN()
	}
	s := append([]float64(nil), values...)
	sort.Float64s(s)
	if p <= 0 {
		return s[0]
	}
	if p >= 100 {
		return s[len(s)-1]
	}
	rank := p / 100 * float64(len(s)-1)
	lo := int(math.Floor(rank))
	hi := int(math.Ceil(rank))
	frac := rank - float64(lo)
	return s[lo]*(1-frac) + s[hi]*frac
}

func Mean(values []float64) float64 {
	if len(values) == 0 {
		return math.NaN()
	}
	var sum float64
	for _, v := range values {
		sum += v
	}
	return sum / float64(len(values))
}

pub fn mean_and_variance(xs: &[f64]) -> Option<(f64, f64)> {
    if xs.len() < 2 {
        return None;
    }
    // Welford's online algorithm: numerically stable single pass.
    let (mut n, mut mean, mut m2) = (0.0_f64, 0.0_f64, 0.0_f64);
    for &x in xs {
        n += 1.0;
        let delta = x - mean;
        mean += delta / n;
        m2 += delta * (x - mean);
    }
    Some((mean, m2 / (n - 1.0)))
}

pub fn histogram(xs: &[f64], bins: usize, lo: f64, hi: f64) -> Vec<usize> {
    let mut out = vec![0usize; bins];
    if bins == 0 || hi <= lo {
        return out;
    }
    let width = (hi - lo) / bins as f64;
    for &x in xs {
        if x < lo || x > hi {
            continue;
        }
        let idx = (((x - lo) / width) as usize).min(bins - 1);
        out[idx] += 1;
    }
    out
}

=== Conversation 7: Probability puzzle ===

User: I have a bag with 5 red marbles and 3 blue marbles. I draw two marbles without replacement. What's the probability both are red? And what's the probability that exactly one is blue?

Assistant: Let's work through it with fractions.

There are 8 marbles in total.

Probability that both are red:
- The first marble is red with probability 5/8.
- Given that, 4 red marbles remain out of 7, so the second is red with probability 4/7.
- Multiply: (5/8) x (4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14, which is about 0.357.

Probability that exactly one is blue:
There are two ways: red then blue, or blue then red.
- Red then blue: (5/8) x (3/7) = 15/56.
- Blue then red: (3/8) x (5/7) = 15/56.
- Total: 30/56 = 15/28, which is about 0.536.

The combination approach is often quicker when you're drawing several items at once, because you count groups rather than sequences. The sequential approach is handy when the draws are described in order or when the conditional structure is part of the question.

=== Conversation 10: Explaining a concept ===

User: Can you explain how compound interest works like I'm 12?

Assistant: Sure. Imagine you put 100 dollars in a piggy bank at a bank that pays you 10 percent interest every year.

After year one, you earn 10 percent of 100, which is 10 dollars. Now you have 110.

After year two, the bank pays 10 percent on your whole 110, not just the original 100. That's 11 dollars, so you have 121.

After year three, 10 percent of 121 is 12.10, so you have 133.10.

Notice that the extra money you earn gets bigger each year. That's because you're earning interest on your interest. That's compound interest: your money makes money, and then that money makes more money.

If it were simple interest, you'd get only 10 dollars every year no matter what, and after three years you'd have 130 instead of 133.10. The gap looks tiny here, but over 30 or 40 years it becomes enormous, which is why people say it's best to start saving early.

User: So how long until my money doubles?

Assistant: There's a handy shortcut called the rule of 72. Divide 72 by the interest rate, and you get the approximate number of years to double. At 10 percent, 72 / 10 = 7.2, so about 7 years. At 6 percent, it takes about 12 years. At 2 percent, about 36 years.

It's an approximation, but it's quite accurate for typical rates, and you can do it in your head, which makes it great for quickly sizing up any savings account or loan.

=== Conversation 14: Debugging a SQL query ===

User: This query returns duplicate rows and I can't figure out why.

SELECT o.id, o.created_at, c.name
FROM orders o
JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.created_at >= '2024-01-01';

Assistant: The duplicates come from the join to order_items. An order with three line items matches three rows in order_items, so the order appears three times in the result, even though you aren't selecting any column from that table. The join multiplies rows.

Which fix is right depends on what you need:

If you don't need anything from order_items, remove that join.

If you only want orders that have at least one item, use EXISTS:

SELECT o.id, o.created_at, c.name
FROM orders o
JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
WHERE o.created_at >= '2024-01-01'
  AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM order_items i WHERE i.order_id = o.id);

If you do want item data aggregated, group by the order:

SELECT o.id, o.created_at, c.name, COUNT(*) AS item_count, SUM(i.quantity * i.unit_price) AS total
FROM orders o
JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.id
WHERE o.created_at >= '2024-01-01'
GROUP BY o.id, o.created_at, c.name;

I'd avoid SELECT DISTINCT as a bandaid. It hides the symptom, costs a sort, and can mask a real data problem later.

Assistant: Usually yes, and often much faster for large tables, because the database can stop searching as soon as it finds one matching item per order, instead of building every combined row and then deduplicating. === Conversation 19: Explaining a bug in a regular expression ===

User: My regex for matching email addresses is /\w+@\w+\.com/. It misses some. Why?

Assistant: Your pattern is very strict, so it fails on lots of legitimate addresses. Here are the main gaps:

1. \w matches letters, digits, and underscore only. Addresses such as first.last@example.com or user+tag@example.com contain dots and plus signs that \w doesn't match.
2. The domain part allows only a single word before ".com". Subdomains such as mail.example.com won't match.
3. It requires ".com". Others like .org, .io, and .co.uk won't match.

A more permissive pattern for simple validation is:

/^[A-Za-z0-9._%+-]+@[A-Za-z0-9.-]+\.[A-Za-z]{2,}$/

This allows dots, plus signs, and hyphens in the local part, and allows subdomains and any top-level domain of two or more letters. It's still not a perfect implementation of the email standard, which is surprisingly complicated, but it's good enough for catching typos in a form.

My strong recommendation is not to depend on regex alone to validate an email. The only real proof that an address works is sending a confirmation message. Use the regex to catch obvious typos and let the confirmation link do the rest.

User: Should I also lowercase it?

Assistant: Generally you should normalize by trimming whitespace and lowercasing the domain part, which is case-insensitive by definition. The local part is technically case-sensitive per the standard, but virtually every mail provider treats it as case-insensitive, so many applications lowercase the entire address to avoid duplicate accounts. If you do so, apply it consistently both when storing and when looking up addresses.

=== Conversation 21: A conceptual machine learning question ===

User: What's the difference between overfitting and underfitting? I always mix them up.

Assistant: Think of it as studying for an exam.

Underfitting is like skimming the textbook for five minutes. You haven't learned enough, so you do badly on practice problems and on the real test. A model that underfits is too simple for the data: it has high error on both the training set and new data. A straight line fitted to data that clearly curves is the classic example.

Overfitting is like memorizing the exact answers to the practice problems without understanding the ideas. You get a perfect score on the practice set, but a different question stumps you. A model that overfits has very low training error but much higher error on new data, because it has fit the noise and quirks of the training set rather than the underlying pattern.

Here's the diagnostic. Compare training and validation error:
- Both high: underfitting. Try a more expressive model, more features, or longer training.
- Training low, validation much higher: overfitting. Try more data, regularization, dropout, early stopping, or a simpler model.
- Both low and close to each other: you're in good shape.

User: So how does regularization help exactly?

Assistant: Regularization adds a penalty for complexity to the training objective, which discourages the model from fitting noise. In linear and neural models, the most common form is L2 regularization (also called weight decay), which adds a term proportional to the sum of squared weights. Large weights are what let a model bend sharply to match individual training points, so penalizing them yields smoother functions.

L1 regularization penalizes the sum of absolute values instead, which tends to drive many weights exactly to zero and therefore performs a kind of feature selection.

Other techniques achieve a similar goal differently: dropout randomly disables units during training so that the network can't depend on any single path, and early stopping halts training when validation error starts to rise. In practice you often combine several, and you tune the strength of each using a validation set.

=== Conversation 23: Docker networking ===

User: I have two containers, a web app and a Postgres database, started with separate docker run commands. The web app can't connect to the database at localhost:5432. Why not?

Assistant: Inside a container, "localhost" refers to the container itself, not your host machine and not other containers. So your web app is looking for Postgres inside its own network namespace, where nothing is listening on 5432.

The cleanest solution is to put both containers on a user-defined network and refer to the database by its container name:

docker network create appnet
docker run -d --name db --network appnet -e POSTGRES_PASSWORD=secret postgres:16
docker run -d --name web --network appnet -p 8080:8080 -e DATABASE_URL=postgres://postgres:secret@db:5432/postgres myapp

On a user-defined bridge network, Docker provides DNS so "db" resolves to the database container's IP. You don't need to publish port 5432 to the host unless you also want to connect from your own machine.

An even easier approach is Docker Compose, where services in the same file share a default network, and you can reference them by service name:

services:
  db:
    image: postgres:16
    environment:
      POSTGRES_PASSWORD: secret
  web:
    build: .
    ports:
      - "8080:8080"
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgres://postgres:secret@db:5432/postgres
    depends_on:
      - db

One caveat: depends_on only controls start order, not readiness. Postgres may take a few seconds to accept connections, so make your app retry on startup, or add a healthcheck and use the condition: service_healthy form.

User: That fixed it. Also my data disappears when I recreate the db container. Normal?

Assistant: Yes, that's expected. A container's filesystem is discarded when the container is removed. To persist data, mount a volume at Postgres's data directory:

docker run -d --name db --network appnet -v pgdata:/var/lib/postgresql/data -e POSTGRES_PASSWORD=secret postgres:16

The named volume pgdata lives on the host and survives container removal. In Compose, declare it under a top-level volumes key and mount it in the service. Just be careful with docker compose down -v, which deletes volumes too.

=== Conversation 26: A philosophical aside ===

User: Do you think a machine could ever really understand language, or is it just pattern matching?

Assistant: It's a debated question, and I'd rather lay out the considerations than pretend there's a settled answer.

One view holds that understanding requires more than manipulating symbols according to statistical regularities. John Searle's Chinese Room argument, for instance, imagines a person following rules to produce fluent Chinese replies without comprehending any of it. On this view, no amount of symbol manipulation yields meaning.

Another view responds that "pattern matching" undersells what it takes to predict language well. To predict text accurately across topics, a system may have to build internal models of the situations that language describes: objects, causes, people's goals. Evidence from interpretability research suggests that trained networks do develop structured internal representations, though how closely these resemble human comprehension is unclear.

A third, more pragmatic stance sets aside the metaphysical question and asks what the system can reliably do: answer novel questions, follow instructions, notice contradictions, and explain its reasoning. By those measures, current systems show real capabilities and real limitations, including confident errors that a human with real understanding might avoid.

ENGINEERING DESIGN DOCUMENT

Title: Moving Notification Delivery to a Queue-Backed Pipeline
Authors: R. Okafor, L. Brandt
Status: Draft for review
Last updated: 14 March

1. Summary

Today our application sends email, push, and SMS notifications synchronously inside the request that triggers them. This was a reasonable shortcut when we sent a few thousand messages a day. We now send around 2.4 million a day, and the shortcut has become the single largest contributor to tail latency on our write endpoints and the most common cause of duplicate sends. This document proposes moving delivery into an asynchronous pipeline built around a durable queue, with per-channel workers, idempotency keys, and a unified retry policy.

We are not proposing to change the notification templates, preference center, or provider contracts. The scope is limited to how a notification moves from "the application decided to send this" to "a provider accepted it."

2. Background and Problems

The current flow works as follows. A request handler, for example the one that processes a new comment, calls a helper that renders the template, looks up the recipient's preferences, and then calls the provider's HTTP API directly. If the provider call takes four seconds, the user's request takes four seconds. If the provider returns an error, the handler either swallows it, which loses the notification, or raises it, which fails the user's original action even though the comment was saved.

We have identified four concrete problems.

Latency coupling. The 99th percentile of our POST /comments endpoint is 3.8 seconds, and 71 percent of that tail is attributable to provider calls. Median latency is a healthy 90 milliseconds.

Duplicate sends. When a handler times out after the provider accepted the message, the client retries and the notification is sent again. In the last quarter, support logged 212 tickets about duplicate emails.

Silent loss. Handlers that swallow provider errors leave no record. We estimate between 0.3 and 0.8 percent of notifications vanish, but we cannot be more precise because we do not record attempts.

Inconsistent retries. The email path retries three times with a fixed one second delay, the SMS path does not retry at all, and the push path retries until the request times out. There is no shared policy.

3. Goals and Non-Goals

Goals:
- Remove provider latency from user-facing requests entirely.
- Guarantee at-least-once delivery to the provider, with deduplication that makes the user-visible effect exactly-once in the common failure modes.
- Record every attempt and its outcome so that support can answer "was this sent?" in under a minute.
- Provide one retry policy with per-channel overrides.
- Allow us to degrade gracefully when a provider is down, by buffering instead of failing.

Non-goals:
- Replacing providers, or building multi-provider failover. We note it as future work.
- Changing the user-facing preference model.
- Real-time delivery guarantees. A target of "within 10 seconds at the 99th percentile" is sufficient.

4. Proposed Design

4.1 Overview

The request handler writes a row to a notifications_outbox table in the same database transaction as the business change. A small relay process reads committed outbox rows and publishes them to the queue. Channel workers consume from per-channel queues, render and send the message, and record the result. This is the classic transactional outbox pattern; it avoids the failure mode where the database commit succeeds but the queue publish fails, or the reverse.

4.2 Outbox schema

Each row carries an idempotency key, the channel, the recipient identifier, the template name, a JSON payload, a status, an attempt count, and timestamps. The idempotency key is derived from the triggering event, for example "comment:8841:recipient:2209:email", so that a retried request produces the same key and the unique constraint rejects the duplicate insert.

4.3 Queue

We evaluated three options.

Option A: a managed message queue from our cloud provider. Pros: no operational burden, built-in dead-letter queues, visibility timeouts. Cons: per-message cost at our volume is roughly 1,100 dollars a month, and ordering guarantees are weak.

Option B: Kafka, which we already run for analytics. Pros: no additional infrastructure, strong ordering per partition. Cons: our cluster is sized for analytics, consumer group semantics are awkward for per-message retries with backoff, and the team that owns it has asked us not to add latency-sensitive workloads.

Option C: using the Postgres outbox table itself as the queue, with workers claiming rows using SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED. Pros: simplest to operate, transactional by construction, fewer moving parts. Cons: it will not scale beyond tens of millions of messages a day without partitioning, and polling adds load to the primary.

We recommend Option A for the delivery queue and keep the outbox as the source of truth. The relay is the only component that touches both. This is slightly more machinery than Option C, but it lets us scale workers independently and keeps polling load off the primary database.

4.4 Workers

There is one worker deployment per channel so that a slow SMS provider cannot starve email. Each worker follows the same loop:

1. Receive a message and read its outbox row.
2. If the row's status is already "sent", acknowledge and stop. This is the idempotency check.
3. Render the template and call the provider, passing the idempotency key in the provider's own deduplication header when one exists.
4. On success, mark the row "sent" and record the provider message id.
5. On a retryable error, such as a timeout or a 5xx response, release the message back to the queue with a delay computed by the retry policy.
6. On a permanent error, such as an invalid address, mark the row "failed" with the reason and acknowledge.

4.5 Retry policy

The default policy is exponential backoff with full jitter, starting at 5 seconds, doubling each attempt, capped at 15 minutes, with a maximum of 8 attempts. After the final attempt the message goes to a dead-letter queue and an alert fires if the dead-letter depth exceeds 50. The SMS channel overrides the maximum to 4 attempts because delayed texts that arrive hours late are worse than no text at all. Push notifications expire after 30 minutes and are dropped rather than retried beyond that.

4.6 Observability

Every state transition emits a structured log line and a metric. The dashboards we plan to build show queue depth per channel, age of the oldest message, send success rate, provider latency percentiles, and dead-letter depth. An admin page lets support search by recipient or idempotency key and view the full attempt history.

5. Rollout Plan

We will roll out in four stages, each gated by an explicit check.

Stage 1, shadow mode (week 1). The handlers keep sending synchronously, and also write outbox rows. Workers run but do not call providers; they compare what they would have sent against what was sent. We expect near-total agreement and will investigate every mismatch.

Stage 2, email at 10 percent (week 2). Email is the lowest-risk channel. We route 10 percent of email through the pipeline, selected by a hash of the recipient id, and watch duplicate rate and latency.

Stage 3, all channels at 50 percent (weeks 3 and 4).

Stage 4, 100 percent and removal of the synchronous path (week 5 onward). We keep a feature flag to fall back for two more weeks.

6. Risks and Mitigations

The relay becomes a single point of failure. Mitigation: run two replicas with leader election, and alert when the oldest unpublished outbox row is older than 60 seconds.

Duplicate delivery during provider timeouts that actually succeeded. Mitigation: provider deduplication keys where supported, and for providers without them, a short-window check against recorded provider message ids.

Backlog after a long provider outage could produce a flood of stale notifications when the provider recovers. Mitigation: per-template expiry times, so a "your code is 481 220" message is dropped after ten minutes while a weekly digest can wait a day.

Cost overrun. Mitigation: we will track per-message cost in the dashboards and revisit if projected spend exceeds 1,500 dollars a month.

7. Open Questions

- Do we need ordering guarantees between notifications to the same recipient? Our current view is no, but product should confirm for the "payment failed" followed by "payment recovered" case.
- Should the admin page allow manual resend? Support has asked for it, but resends bypass idempotency by definition and need an audit trail.
- What is the retention period for outbox rows? We propose 90 days online and archival to object storage afterward.

8. Alternatives Considered and Rejected

Fire-and-forget background threads inside the web process: rejected because notifications would be lost on every deploy and on every crash.

A third-party notification orchestration service: rejected for now due to cost and data-residency review time, though it deserves a second look if we add multi-provider failover.

POSTMORTEM

Incident: Partial checkout outage caused by an expired internal certificate
Date: 9 November
Duration: 1 hour 52 minutes (14:07 to 15:59 UTC)
Severity: SEV-2
Author: M. Delacroix
Status: Final, reviewed

Impact

During the incident, roughly 38 percent of checkout attempts failed with a generic error message. Customers who retried had a decent chance of success, which made the issue look intermittent and delayed diagnosis. We estimate 4,100 failed checkouts, of which approximately 1,500 were abandoned permanently, representing around 61,000 dollars in delayed or lost revenue. No customer data was exposed or corrupted.

Timeline (all times UTC)

14:07 The internal TLS certificate used for communication between the payments service and the fraud-scoring service expired. Three of the eight fraud-scoring instances had been restarted earlier in the day and picked up the new certificate, so five instances rejected connections and three accepted them.
14:12 Error rate on checkout crossed the alert threshold of 5 percent. The on-call engineer, J. Park, was paged.
14:20 J. Park began investigating the payments service and saw connection errors, but the messages said only "handshake failure." The initial suspicion was a network issue following a maintenance window in the datacenter earlier that morning.
14:41 The network team confirmed no ongoing maintenance and no packet loss. The investigation shifted to a possible bad deploy. A deploy had gone out at 11:30; the team prepared a rollback.
15:02 The rollback completed but the error rate did not change. This ruled out the deploy.
15:14 A second engineer, T. Nakamura, noticed that the failures were correlated with specific fraud-scoring instances. Examining one failing instance's certificate with openssl showed the expiry date had passed at 14:07.
15:31 A new certificate was issued manually from the internal certificate authority.
15:48 The certificate was distributed and the failing instances were restarted in a rolling manner.
15:59 Error rate returned to baseline. Incident closed.

Root Cause

The certificate had a one-year lifetime and was issued manually by an engineer who has since left the company. The renewal reminder was a calendar entry in that person's personal calendar. No automated expiry monitoring existed for internal certificates, and the instance-level inconsistency, caused by restarts, made the failure look like a flaky network.

Contributing Factors

1. Certificate lifecycle was undocumented and depended on a single individual.
2. The error message "handshake failure" did not say that the peer certificate had expired, because the client library hid the underlying reason by default.
3. The partial failure pattern led us toward network and deploy hypotheses, both of which consumed time.
4. Our dashboards showed aggregate error rates but not error rates by upstream instance, which would have revealed the correlation immediately.

What Went Well

- The alert fired within five minutes of impact beginning.
- Communication was good: status page updates went out every 20 minutes, and the support team had accurate talking points.
- The rollback procedure worked smoothly even though it turned out to be unnecessary.

What Went Poorly

- It took over an hour to look at the certificate, which is a standard first check for handshake failures.
- We did not have a checklist for TLS errors.
- The manual certificate issue process took 17 minutes because the documentation was out of date.

Action Items

1. Deploy automated certificate management using short-lived certificates renewed every 24 hours. Owner: platform team. Due: 30 November.
2. Add an alert for any certificate within 21 days of expiry, including internal ones, using the existing exporter. Owner: T. Nakamura. Due: 16 November.
3. Configure the client library to log the verification failure reason. Owner: J. Park. Due: 20 November.
4. Add per-upstream-instance error rate to the checkout dashboard. Owner: M. Delacroix. Due: 23 November.
5. Write a runbook for TLS handshake failures, with the first steps being checking certificate expiry and chain. Owner: J. Park. Due: 27 November.
6. Inventory all certificates in use, with owners, expiry dates, and renewal procedures. Owner: platform team. Due: 14 December.

Lessons

The most useful lesson is about the shape of the failure. A problem that affects only some instances almost always points to a difference between instances, such as configuration, version, or certificate, rather than to a shared dependency. Had we asked "what differs between the failing and passing hosts?" in the first fifteen minutes, we would have shortened this incident by more than an hour. We have added that question to the incident response checklist.

A SMALL COLLECTION OF RECIPES

Lemon Ricotta Pancakes

Makes about 12 pancakes, serves 4.

Ingredients: 1 cup (240 g) whole milk ricotta, 3 large eggs separated, 3/4 cup (95 g) all-purpose flour, 2 tablespoons sugar, zest of 1 large lemon plus 2 tablespoons of juice, 1/2 teaspoon baking powder, 1/4 teaspoon salt, butter for the pan.

Method. In a large bowl, whisk the ricotta, egg yolks, lemon zest, and lemon juice until smooth. Stir in the flour, sugar, baking powder, and salt just until combined; a few lumps are fine. In a separate clean bowl, beat the egg whites until they hold soft peaks. Fold a third of the whites into the batter to loosen it, then fold in the rest gently so you keep the air.

Heat a nonstick skillet over medium-low heat and melt a little butter. Spoon about a quarter cup of batter per pancake and cook until the edges look set and the bottoms are golden, about three minutes. Flip and cook two minutes more. These are tender, so use a thin spatula and don't crowd the pan. Keep finished pancakes warm in a low oven while you cook the rest. Serve with berries and a little honey.

Weeknight Chickpea and Spinach Stew

Serves 4 with rice or crusty bread.

Ingredients: 2 tablespoons olive oil, 1 large onion diced, 4 cloves garlic minced, 1 tablespoon tomato paste, 2 teaspoons ground cumin, 1 teaspoon smoked paprika, 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon, a pinch of chili flakes, 2 cans (400 g each) chickpeas drained, 1 can (400 g) crushed tomatoes, 2 cups vegetable stock, 200 g fresh spinach, salt, and a squeeze of lemon.

Method. Warm the oil in a heavy pot over medium heat and cook the onion with a pinch of salt for about eight minutes, until soft and starting to color. Add the garlic, tomato paste, and spices and stir for one minute until fragrant. Add the chickpeas, tomatoes, and stock. Bring to a simmer and cook uncovered for 20 minutes, stirring now and then, until the sauce thickens. Mash a few chickpeas against the side of the pot to give the stew body. Stir in the spinach in handfuls until wilted. Taste and adjust the salt, then finish with lemon juice. It tastes even better the next day.

Overnight Oats, Three Ways

The base for one jar: 1/2 cup rolled oats, 1/2 cup milk of your choice, 1/4 cup plain yogurt, 1 teaspoon honey or maple syrup, pinch of salt. Stir together in a jar, cover, and refrigerate for at least six hours.

Apple pie version: add 1/4 cup grated apple, 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon, and a spoonful of chopped walnuts in the morning.
Tropical version: add 1/4 cup diced mango, a tablespoon of shredded coconut, and a splash of lime juice.
Chocolate banana version: add 1 tablespoon cocoa powder to the base overnight and top with sliced banana and a few chocolate chips.

Classic Vinaigrette

Whisk together 1 tablespoon Dijon mustard, 2 tablespoons red wine vinegar, 1 small minced shallot, a pinch of salt, and black pepper. Slowly stream in 6 tablespoons of olive oil while whisking until the mixture emulsifies. The mustard acts as the emulsifier, holding oil and vinegar together. Taste on a leaf of lettuce, not on a spoon, because that's how it will actually be eaten. Keeps in the refrigerator for a week.

MATHEMATICS NOTES

1. The quadratic formula and the discriminant

For the equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$, completing the square gives
$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.$$
The quantity $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ determines the nature of the roots: two distinct real roots when $\Delta > 0$, one repeated real root when $\Delta = 0$, and a conjugate pair of complex roots when $\Delta < 0$. By Vieta's formulas, the roots $r_1, r_2$ satisfy $r_1 + r_2 = -b/a$ and $r_1 r_2 = c/a$.

2. A geometric series

For $|r| < 1$,
$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} r^k = \frac{1}{1 - r}.$$
To see this, let $S_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} r^k$. Then $S_n - rS_n = 1 - r^{n+1}$, so $S_n = \dfrac{1 - r^{n+1}}{1 - r}$, and as $n \to \infty$ the term $r^{n+1}$ vanishes. For example, with $r = 1/2$ the sum is $2$, and with $r = -1/3$ it is $3/4$.

3. The Gaussian integral

One of the most remarkable results in analysis is
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}.$$
The standard proof squares the integral and switches to polar coordinates:
$$I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\, r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \pi.$$
Taking the positive square root gives $I = \sqrt{\pi}$. This result underlies the normalization of the normal distribution, whose density is
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

4. Eigenvalues of a small matrix

Consider
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The characteristic polynomial is $\det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$, so the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$. An eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 5$ solves $(A - 5I)v = 0$, which gives $-v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $v = (1, 1)^T$. For $\lambda_2 = 2$ we get $2v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $v = (1, -2)^T$. As a check, $\operatorname{tr}(A) = 7 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$ and $\det(A) = 10 = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$.

5. Bayes' theorem and a medical test

Let $D$ be the event that a person has a disease and $T$ a positive test. Then
$$P(D \mid T) = \frac{P(T \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(T \mid D)\,P(D) + P(T \mid \neg D)\,P(\neg D)}.$$
Suppose the prevalence is $P(D) = 0.01$, the sensitivity is $P(T \mid D) = 0.95$, and the false positive rate is $P(T \mid \neg D) = 0.05$. Then
$$P(D \mid T) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.0095}{0.0590} \approx 0.161.$$
Despite a seemingly accurate test, only about sixteen percent of positive results are true positives, because the disease is rare.

6. Euler's identity and a series for e

The exponential function has the series $e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{x^n}{n!}$. Substituting $x = i\theta$ and separating real and imaginary parts gives Euler's formula, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. Setting $\theta = \pi$ yields
$$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.$$

7. A limit and a derivative

The fundamental limit
$$\lim_{n \to \infty}\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = e$$
is the standard definition of Euler's number. Using it, one can show that $\dfrac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$ and, by the chain rule, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}$. For the product of two functions, the derivative is $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$, so for example $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(x^2 \sin x\right) = 2x\sin x + x^2 \cos x$.

8. Sum of the first n integers, by induction

Claim: $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ for all integers $n \ge 1$.

Base case: for $n = 1$ the left side is $1$ and the right side is $\frac{1 \cdot 2}{2} = 1$.

Inductive step: assume the claim holds for $n$. Then
$$\sum_{k=1}^{n+1} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + (n+1) = \frac{n(n+1) + 2(n+1)}{2} = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2},$$
which is the claim for $n+1$. By induction, the formula holds for every positive integer.

9. Variance and the central limit theorem

If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, then the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ has $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2 / n$. The central limit theorem states that
$$\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1).$$
In practice this means that for moderately large $n$, the distribution of the average is approximately normal regardless of the shape of the underlying distribution, which is why normal-based confidence intervals work so widely.

TECHNICAL NOTE: CHOOSING A CACHE INVALIDATION STRATEGY

Audience: backend engineers on the catalog team
Purpose: record why we settled on versioned keys instead of explicit deletes

The catalog service caches product pages for 24 hours. Until recently, an edit to a product triggered a delete of the matching cache entry. That worked until the product grew variants, localized descriptions, and a related-items panel. A single edit could now affect forty cache entries, and the code that enumerated them lived in three places that rarely agreed with each other. Twice in the last quarter, a price change appeared on the product page but not in the search results, because one of the keys had been forgotten.

Three strategies were considered.

Explicit deletion. This is what we had. It is the most intuitive approach and it gives precise control, but it demands that every writer know every reader. As the number of cached views grows, the knowledge required grows with it, and mistakes produce stale data with no error. The failure is silent, which is the worst kind.

Short time-to-live. Setting the expiry to sixty seconds bounds staleness without any coordination. The cost is a much lower hit rate. When we simulated the change against a day of production traffic, the hit rate fell from 94 percent to 71 percent, and database read load roughly quadrupled. We rejected it as a primary strategy, though we may use it as a safety net.

Versioned keys. Each product carries a monotonically increasing version number stored in the database. Every cache key embeds the version, for example product:8841:v17:en. An edit increments the version, and from that moment on readers ask for a key that does not exist yet, so they miss and repopulate. The old entries are never deleted; they simply stop being asked for, and they expire naturally. The writer needs to know nothing about readers.

The versioned approach has one subtle requirement. The reader must learn the current version cheaply, otherwise we have only moved the database read. We solved this by keeping the version in a small, separate cache with a five second expiry. This bounds the staleness window to five seconds, which product accepted, and it keeps the hot path to two cache reads.

There is also a consequence for memory. Because stale entries linger until expiry, peak memory use rises by about 12 percent in a heavy editing period. We judged this acceptable. If it becomes a problem, the cache can be configured to evict least recently used entries first, which would remove the orphaned versions early since nobody reads them.

Summary of the decision: versioned keys, a five second version cache, and a sixty minute TTL on content entries as a backstop. We will revisit if product requires immediate consistency for prices, in which case the price field should probably be fetched uncached from a dedicated service.

ON-CALL RUNBOOK EXCERPT: DISK SPACE ALERTS

Symptom. The alert "disk usage above 85 percent" fires for a database or log host.

First, determine which filesystem is affected and how quickly it is growing. Run df with the human-readable flag, then compare against yesterday's value in the dashboard. A steady creep over weeks is a capacity problem; a sudden jump of ten points in an hour is an incident.

Second, find the consumer. On the affected host, run du with a depth limit of one directory against the mount, sort by size, and descend into the largest entry. The usual suspects, in order of how often they turn out to be the cause:

- Log files that have stopped rotating. Check that the rotation service ran. If a single log is larger than five gigabytes, compress or truncate it after confirming with the service owner that it is not needed. Truncating a file that a process holds open is safe; deleting it is not, because the space will not be released until the process closes it.
- Database write-ahead logs accumulating because a replica has fallen behind or a replication slot is orphaned. Check replication lag first. If a slot has no consumer, drop it, but only after confirming with the data team.
- Core dumps from a crash-looping service. Look in the crash directory and note the timestamps. Fix the crash, not the disk.
- Temporary files from a long-running export job.

Third, buy time if the situation is urgent. Moving old logs to object storage frees space immediately and is reversible. Do not delete database files by hand under any circumstances.

Fourth, follow up. Whatever the cause, open a ticket describing it and attach the dashboard graphs. If the cause was capacity, estimate the date at which the disk would fill at the current growth rate and put that date in the ticket title.

Escalation. If usage passes 95 percent and you have not found the cause within fifteen minutes, page the secondary on-call and the database owner. Writes may begin to fail at 98 percent.

FIELD NOTES: A WEEKEND IN THE LOWLAND MARSHES

Saturday. We left the car at the end of the gravel track a little after seven and walked out along the sea wall with the sun just clearing the line of pylons. The tide was out, and the mudflats shone like hammered pewter. There were redshanks everywhere, working the edges of the channels with that nervous, bobbing walk, and every few minutes something would spook them and the whole flock would lift, wheel once, and settle fifty yards further along as if nothing had happened.

By nine we had logged forty-one species, including a pair of avocets that we nearly missed because they were standing perfectly still in the glare, and a marsh harrier quartering the reeds so low that its shadow trailed it across the water. A man with a very large lens told us that a spotted redshank had been seen the day before near the second hide, so we went there and waited. The hide smelled of damp wood and old flasks of tea. Nobody spoke above a whisper. After forty minutes, a dark, long-legged shape stepped out of the reeds, and the man with the lens made a small happy noise that I will remember for a long time.

Sunday. Rain, then fog. The marsh without visibility is a different place, all sound: the rattling call of a water rail somewhere unseen, the cough of a distant pheasant, the soft ticking of rain on the reeds. We walked the inland path and found a barn owl asleep in a hollow willow, one eye barely open, entirely unbothered by us. On the way back we crossed a field of lapwings, and one of them performed the tumbling display flight, rolling over and over in the grey air above us as though gravity were a thing it had read about.

We drove home with the heater on, the windows fogged, and the notebook between us on the seat, a little muddy and, I think, a little changed.

MORE RECIPES

Roasted Tomato Soup

Serves 4.

Halve 1 kilogram of ripe tomatoes and place them cut-side up on a baking tray with one head of garlic split across its middle, one quartered onion, a generous drizzle of olive oil, salt, and a teaspoon of sugar. Roast at 200 C for 45 minutes, until the edges are charred and the juices have caramelized. Squeeze the softened garlic from its skin into a pot and add the tomatoes, onion, and all the roasting juices, plus 500 ml of stock and a handful of basil. Simmer for ten minutes, then blend until smooth. If the soup tastes sharp, add a small knob of butter or a pinch more sugar. Serve with grilled cheese sandwiches for dipping, as the universe intended.

Quick Pickled Red Onions

Slice two red onions thinly and pack into a jar. In a small saucepan, warm 3/4 cup white wine vinegar, 3/4 cup water, 1 tablespoon sugar, and 2 teaspoons salt until the sugar and salt dissolve. Pour over the onions, pressing them down so they are submerged. After thirty minutes they are pink and crisp; after a day they are better. They keep for two weeks in the refrigerator and improve tacos, salads, sandwiches, and rice bowls.

Dark Chocolate Olive Oil Cake

Serves 8 to 10.

Preheat the oven to 175 C and line a 23 cm round pan. Melt 150 g of dark chocolate with 120 ml of olive oil, stirring until smooth, and let it cool slightly. In a bowl, whisk 3 eggs with 150 g of sugar until pale and thick, about three minutes. Fold in the chocolate mixture, then 60 g of almond flour, 30 g of flour, 1/2 teaspoon of baking powder, and 1/2 teaspoon of salt. Pour into the pan and bake for 28 to 32 minutes, until the edges are set and the center is just barely wobbling. Cool completely before turning out. Dust with powdered sugar, and serve with whipped cream and a pinch of flaky salt.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS: LIBRARY MEMBERSHIP

How do I get a library card? Bring a photo ID and a document showing your current address to any branch. If you live in the county, the card is free and is issued on the spot. You can also pre-register online and collect the card at a branch within thirty days.

How many items can I borrow? Up to fifty items at a time, including no more than ten DVDs and five items from the new releases shelf. Digital loans do not count toward the limit.

How long can I keep things? Books and audiobooks are loaned for three weeks. DVDs and video games are loaned for one week. Most items can be renewed twice, unless another member has placed a hold on them.

Are there late fees? No. The library stopped charging daily fines in the spring. If an item is more than thirty days overdue, your account is paused until it is returned, and a replacement fee may apply if the item is not returned within ninety days.

Can I reserve a room? Study rooms can be booked up to two weeks in advance through the online calendar, for up to two hours per day. Larger meeting rooms are available to community groups; ask at the front desk for the application form.

What about accessibility? All branches are step-free, with automatic doors and accessible restrooms. Large print books, audiobooks, and assistive reading devices are available on request. Staff can also arrange sign language interpreters for events with two weeks' notice.

MORE MATHEMATICS

10. The Fibonacci sequence and the golden ratio

Define $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$, and $F_{n+2} = F_{n+1} + F_n$. Solving the characteristic equation $x^2 = x + 1$ yields the roots $\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$ and $\psi = \frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}$, and Binet's formula
$$F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}.$$
Since $|\psi| < 1$, the second term shrinks rapidly, so $F_n$ is the nearest integer to $\varphi^n/\sqrt{5}$. The ratio $F_{n+1}/F_n$ converges to $\varphi \approx 1.6180339887$.

11. Integration by parts

From the product rule one obtains
$$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du.$$
For instance, with $u = x$ and $dv = e^x\,dx$, we have $du = dx$ and $v = e^x$, so
$$\int x e^x\,dx = x e^x - \int e^x\,dx = (x - 1)e^x + C.$$
A second example, with a definite integral: $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} x\sin x\,dx = \left[-x\cos x\right]_0^{\pi} + \int_0^{\pi}\cos x\,dx = \pi + 0 = \pi$.

12. Basic counting

The number of ways to choose $k$ items from $n$ without regard to order is
$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}.$$
These satisfy Pascal's rule, $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$, and the binomial theorem
$$(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^k.$$
Setting $x = y = 1$ shows that $\sum_{k}\binom{n}{k} = 2^n$, which agrees with the observation that a set with $n$ elements has $2^n$ subsets.

13. Gradient descent on a quadratic

Let $f(w) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,w^{\top} A w - b^{\top} w$ with $A$ symmetric positive definite. The gradient is $\nabla f(w) = Aw - b$, and the update rule is
$$w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta\,(A w_t - b).$$
Writing $e_t = w_t - w^*$ where $w^* = A^{-1}b$, we get $e_{t+1} = (I - \eta A)\,e_t$. The iteration converges when $0 < \eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(A)$, and the best fixed step size is $\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda_{\max})$, giving a contraction factor of $\dfrac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$ where $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the condition number.

14. Softmax and cross-entropy

For logits $z \in \mathbb{R}^K$, the softmax is $p_i = \dfrac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$. The cross-entropy loss against a true class $y$ is $\mathcal{L} = -\log p_y = -z_y + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}$, and its gradient with respect to the logits is $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z_i = p_i - \mathbb{1}[i = y]$. For numerical stability, one subtracts $\max_j z_j$ from every logit before exponentiating, which leaves the probabilities unchanged.

15. Kullback-Leibler divergence

For discrete distributions $P$ and $Q$ on the same support,
$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{x} P(x)\,\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}.$$
It is nonnegative by Jensen's inequality, equals zero exactly when $P = Q$, and is not symmetric. If $P = (0.5, 0.5)$ and $Q = (0.9, 0.1)$, then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) = 0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.9} + 0.5\ln\frac{0.5}{0.1} \approx 0.5(-0.588) + 0.5(1.609) \approx 0.511$ nats.
